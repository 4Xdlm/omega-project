To seize in everything "the irresistible
progress toward death"—this is the
subject of The Grass. The novel is the
story of a young woman who describes
to her lover the death-agony o ran old
woman—her husband's aunt—who
lies in a great dark room in the house
up on the hill. She talks in a deep mon¬
ologue that awakens memory: and
through this conversation we learn
about her past: her existence during
the days of the fall of France, the mar¬
riage of her famous brother, the mon¬
strous jealousy of her sister-in-law, the
insignificance of their son. Her solil¬
oquy ultimately takes issue with the
double enigma that is life—a voice that
resists the mockery that from a pas¬
sionate existence nothing remains ex¬
cept a few lines, a few entries in an ac¬
count book, some jewels—so often
given, hidden, lost, recovered, stolen,
sold, and they too ending in dust. It is
the persistence and permanence of this
voice that relays the power of this novel
and explores the only destiny we can
know with any certainty—the flight
of time.
