Chapter 10

The Photograph


Faith


Morning in Split wore a cleaner kind of danger.


The ferry terminal glared white in the daylight, gulls scribbling curses across a sky too bright to be honest. Engines coughed, ropes groaned, and tourists did what tourists do—turned crime scenes into postcards by not looking hard enough. I stood with a coffee that had the temperament of a weapon and watched Lorenzo become the version of himself daylight preferred: immaculate, cordial, unmovable.


Dante ghosted ahead, a paperback and sunglasses he didn’t need. Rocco leaned on a railing the way mountains lean. Two men I didn’t know became flower sellers by holding the same bundle of carnations for too long.


The Englishman arrived on the nine o’clock dot, which was exactly how men like him said trust me without deserving it. He had a banker’s suit and a priest’s smile and shoes that knew how to step out of things. He carried nothing but a leather folio. He looked at Lorenzo first, me second, and made the mistake of letting that second look last.


“Mr. Ellis,” Dante said without affection.


“Mr. Dan,” the Englishman returned, mangling the name on purpose. His eyes slid back to Lorenzo with the lazy respect of money. “Don Bellucci. An honor.”


“Let’s keep the liturgy short,” Lorenzo said.


They took a bench angled toward the water, a tableau of civility for any camera pointed this way. I stood half a step behind and to his left, exactly where he wanted me: inside the frame, outside the reach. The Englishman set the folio on his knee and folded his hands as if they had never done anything unclean.


“Karras asks for reason,” he began. “The Adriatic is crowded. Crowds cause confusion. Confusion causes loss. Loss—”


“Is paid for,” Lorenzo said smoothly. “Three boxes. Twenty-four hours to return them. Ten percent until corridors stop pretending to be theaters. You came here to agree.”


“I came here,” Ellis said, “to show you something.” He glanced at me once, thin as a razor. “May I?”


Lorenzo didn’t bother to answer. Dante shifted—half a degree, enough for the Englishman to decide his question had been heard and denied. He opened the folio anyway. Paper sighed. A single glossy photograph slid into the light and lay there like a fish yanked from dark water.


My breath did the dumb thing—stopped before the rest of me could decide whether to look. I looked.


Atlanta. Two years ago. A school hallway washed in afternoon. Me in a denim jacket, hand on a boy’s shoulder, smiling the way people do when they still think kindness is currency that spends. The boy’s name bent the back of my teeth. Jaden. He had painted a city on butcher paper with a brush too big for his hand and my heart had cracked at the edges because some children paint escape hatches when they mean doors.


Ellis didn’t smile this time. “America is far but not difficult,” he said softly. “So many strings to pull. So many people willing to be helpful.”


I felt Lorenzo go still. Not the kind of still that looks like indecision; the kind that looks like weather about to choose a city. His gaze never left Ellis. “You came to talk about shipping,” he said. “You showed me that to talk about lines I do not permit crossing.”


“I came to talk about leverage,” Ellis corrected gently. “Who has it, who keeps it, who loses it when they let strangers on their boat. You’ve brought your wife into corridors where photographs travel faster than apologies.”


That last word was a mistake. It put Ellis in the same paragraph as Petrovic, and the morning had already decided who paid for that.


I made myself breathe. I made myself not touch the picture. I made myself remember Marcella’s voice in the lemon garden: Don’t be a mirror. Be glass that cuts. When I spoke, my voice was steady enough to surprise me.


“You found an old hallway,” I said. “Congratulations. That boy has a scholarship now. I wrote the letter. Tell your money he can’t buy that.”


Ellis turned his head enough to feign courtesy. “You Americans do love your stories.”


“We love evidence,” I said. “And yours proves you’re frightened.”


He blinked once, twice, recalculating. Men in his business like balance sheets; they dislike women who move numbers without permission.


Lorenzo tapped the folio with one finger, a touch light as a fuse. “This is not reason,” he said. “This is trespass.”


Ellis’s mouth remembered how to smile. “Depends on the country.”


Dante’s phone buzzed—one short note. He didn’t move. Rocco scratched his jaw with the back of his hand, which meant three where it counted. Two men by the ticket booth had stopped pretending to be passengers. A third was late to his own story, jogging from the far end of the promenade with a jacket too heavy for the weather and a face that didn’t know how to be unremarkable.


“I’m bored,” Matteo said in my ear.


I hadn’t seen him arrive. Of course he had arrived—smelling of adrenaline and a joke he hadn’t told yet. He lounged against the low wall behind us, coin hunting sunlight.


“Leave,” Dante murmured without turning.


“Stay,” Lorenzo said, not to either of them. To me.


Ellis closed the folio and placed his palms on it like a bible he meant to swear on. “You want ten percent, Don Bellucci? Take it. But you don’t bring your… ornament to meetings with men who have nothing to lose.”


“Ornament,” Matteo repeated, delighted. “Say it again.”


Ellis didn’t. He stood. “We’ll return your boxes,” he said. “We’ll keep the corridor neat. Keep your house neater, and we all enjoy profit.”


“Profit is simple,” Lorenzo said. “Etiquette is where men fail.”


The third man—the one running late—reached us then, panting in Croatian about a lost ticket, a missed ferry, his mother. He bumped Ellis’s shoulder with middle-class apology and dropped a folded newspaper.


The newspaper bloomed knives.


It happened like music: a wrong chord and then all the noise. The “passenger” at the booth produced a piece from his waistband; the “tourist” aimed his phone and found a gun instead; the late runner kicked the newspaper—three boxy things wrapped under it—and reached to pull Ellis behind him. He wasn’t saving the Englishman; he was using him as a shield.


Rocco moved first. He had already been moving. His shoulder hit the runner, a low, irresistible wave. The phone-gun went up and sneezed daylight—one ugly pop—and a gull lost a feather. Dante’s paperback snapped shut and became a fist; the booth man’s wrist learned a fast religion. Ellis froze, which is what men do when their job finally looks like what it is.


I didn’t run. I stepped into Ellis, too close for him to decide where his arms ended and mine began, and took the folio. He realized and tried to tug; I didn’t tug back. I met his eyes and smiled like a woman who had just discovered a use for daylight.


“Etiquette,” I said, and let the word cut.


Behind me: shouts, the steel bark of a weapon choking, the slap of shoe on stone. Matteo laughed—he laughed because he always laughed when the world broke—and then there was the sound of someone’s head meeting a bench.


The newspaper’s packages scattered. One slid into shadow, one cracked open and shed wires, the third skittered to the lip of the quay and paused there, thinking about being interesting. Rocco kicked it back toward his own boot and set his heel on it as if it were a beetle that needed considering.


“Stop,” Ellis said. “We’re in public.”


“That’s why you picked it,” Dante said, panting once. “You wanted noise.”


“We brought a broom,” Matteo added, just before he broke the runner’s nose.


The phone-gun misfired into stone. A child’s cry rendered through the air. Tourists decided the ferry this hour wasn’t the one for them. From the edge of my vision I saw a woman in a red coat raise her hands—slowly, cautiously—and back away with that European grace that says I am not here to be helpful.


I kept my eyes on Ellis and the folio under my arm. “You brought Atlanta to this bench,” I said. “That was unwise.”


“You brought it first,” he said. “When you married a man who makes maps for living meat.”


The phrase shouldn’t have landed. It did. Because the photograph still breathed against my ribs, because part of me wanted to go buy a plane ticket for a boy who had painted escape hatches and tell him to never say my name out loud again.


“Faith,” Lorenzo said, low, a calming line across my back. In that single sound: I am here. You are seen. Choose how you want this to end and I will build the room around it.


I chose.


“Walk,” I told Ellis, and took his elbow like a hostess. “We’ll do etiquette properly: away from trash.”


He understood then that I was not ornament. He glanced at Lorenzo, found permission, and let me steer him ten paces along the quay to where the noise became backdrop instead of foreground. Rocco and Dante were always between us and the world anyway; power traveled like that with us now.


“Here is the story you carry back,” I said. “You return the boxes. You pay the ten. You keep your waiter’s friends off the sidewalk. You do not buy photographs of children who have done nothing but trust adults. You do not speak to me again unless I let you. And you tell Karras this corridor has a price he can afford as long as he remembers who owns the road.”


He met my gaze like he regretted two decades of underestimating women. “And if I don’t?”


“You will,” I said. “Or you’ll learn how exact Neapolitans can be.”


His mouth twitched—amusement or respect. “I’ll deliver your sermon,” he said. “Don’t mistake my employer for a man who enjoys pews.”


“Don’t mistake mine for a man who can’t burn a church down and build it again by morning,” I said.


Dante joined us, sleeve darkening at the cuff where someone else’s blood had shown optimism. “Police will want to be called,” he told Ellis in gentle English. “You will be the one to call them. You will say a thief tried to take your wallet and you dropped your newspaper. That is all.”


“And the packages?” Ellis asked lightly.


“Gone,” Dante said. “Like your courage.”


Ellis, to his credit, didn’t bristle. He took his folio back when I offered it, the briefest brush of knuckles acknowledging that I had chosen not to keep it. “Mrs. Bellucci,” he said, just as lightly. “My compliments.”


“Don’t bring me presents again,” I said.


We let him go. He called the police with the conscience of a practiced liar. The half-unwrapped device vanished under Dante’s jacket; the others, Rocco palmed and delivered to a van that looked like it sold bread. Matteo wiped his knuckles on the runner’s jacket and rolled his shoulders, pleased with his morning.


“Next time we plan this for a time when the pigeons are better fed,” he said cheerfully.


“Next time you stay in the car until I say otherwise,” Dante said.


I kept my hand on the folio until Ellis was a bright dot near the taxi rank. Then I breathed and felt my body tremble the way boats tremble after docking—still moving at a speed the eye can’t see.


Lorenzo stood in front of me, blocking the harbor, the city, the hour. His eyes were darker than the sea; daylight didn’t dilute him. “Look at me,” he said softly.


I did. I always did.


“You did well,” he said. “You cut.”


I nodded. The air felt thin. “He had Atlanta,” I said. “Jaden.”


“We will take his cameras from him,” he said. “We will take the men who fetch his pictures. And we will put a wall between our life and the old one that even your name can’t climb.”


“It already climbed,” I said. “He found the boy.”


A pause—for truth, not for comfort. “He found a hallway,” Lorenzo said. “He did not find your beginning. He did not find your end.”


Matteo whistled low. “You two gonna make me cry in public?”


“Go buy a cardigan and practice subtlety,” Dante told him. “You’ll need both this afternoon.”


Rocco spoke into the radio once; the world folded back into place like paper. The tourists returned to their advice about the best gelato. The gulls resumed arguing. The bench remembered it was a bench.


We left the quay as if nothing had happened. It had. The corridor was cleaner. The percentage was agreed. The Englishman had been taught a word in a language he didn’t know how to spell: enough.


Back at the hotel, the suite smelled like salt and coffee and a future we would have to keep buying day by day. I washed my hands and watched clear water turn honest. When I came out, Dante waited at the table with a laptop and the kind of satisfaction he’d never admit to liking.


“Server is ours,” he said. “Every label change. Every invoice. Every polite lie.” He looked at me. “I don’t say this to be kind, Faith. I say it because it’s a number. You made him sloppy.”


“Good,” I said. “I’d like him messier.”


Matteo sprawled on the sofa, shoes on the cushion because that’s what made him feel like he owned rooms. “You want my opinion?” he asked.


“No,” Dante and I said together.


He gave me a grin that almost made me forgive him. “You cut pretty,” he said. “That’s a compliment where we come from.”


“Where we come from,” I said, “children who paint cities get to grow up in them.”


Silence touched the edges of that sentence. Lorenzo crossed to me and threaded his fingers through mine, not spectacle—proof. His voice was meant for me alone. “Tell me what you want.”


“Kill the photograph,” I said. “And the man who sent it, if he tries again.”


He kissed my temple, a vow without violence. “Done.”


The heat between us rose then, a pulse under the day’s harsh light, but I held it back because we had chosen suspense this morning, not solace. He understood. He let the promise sit and warm the room for later.


Dante closed the laptop. “We have three hours before Iliopoulos’s man wants to repent over coffee,” he said. “Matteo, go walk where cameras can see you but not hear you. Rocco, get the car that looks like a mistake. Faith—”


I raised a brow. “Yes?”


He studied me the way he studied maps. Respect first, then warning. “You’re part of the collateral now,” he said quietly. “That isn’t an insult. It’s the truth I count with.”


“I know,” I said.


“Then count this with me,” he said. “If they can’t move you, they will try to move what you touch. Friends. Students. Mothers who brought you coffee in Atlanta. We’ll close those doors before they even find the hallways. But it means we move faster than you want.”


I exhaled, slow. “Close them.”


“We already started,” he said. “You can be angry with me about it later.”


I wasn’t angry. I was something harder and more useful. “Thank you,” I said.


Lorenzo’s thumb climbed my knuckles, a small climb, a familiar summit. “Three hours,” he said. “You rest. Then we finish teaching them why corridors should be clean.”


I nodded. I tried to sit. I couldn’t. I stood at the window and watched Split shimmer like a blade under the noon sun. Somewhere in this city, a man developed photographs and thought that made him dangerous.


I pressed my palm to the glass and let the heat collect. I wasn’t an ornament. I wasn’t a rumor. I was a piece on the board that could move. And moving meant other things would break.


Fine. We would count. We would cut. We would collect.


And if the past came hunting with paper again, we would burn the paper before it turned into fire.

Chapter 11

Burn The Chapel


Lorenzo


The sea looked wrong.


Too calm. Too blue. Too quiet. The kind of quiet that mocked a man who thought control was the same as peace.


Naples had always spoken in noise—horns, curses, prayers—but this morning it whispered. Even the gulls were silent. The storm had passed, and still the air felt heavy with its ghost.


I stood at the window of my study, watching the water grind against the rocks. The reflection in the glass didn’t move for a long time. I had been up since before dawn, waiting for the report I already knew I’d hate.


Dante entered without knocking. He placed a thin folder on the desk, not looking at me until the last page stopped fluttering. “We found the leak.”


“Say it.”


He met my eyes. “Matteo.”


The word hit like gunfire at close range. Short. Loud. Final.


For a second, I heard nothing but the rush of my own pulse. My brother’s name had been a thousand things in my life—a curse, a prayer, a memory of laughter—but never this. Never the sound of betrayal.


“Proof?” I asked.


Dante slid a photo across the desk. Matteo shaking hands with a man I’d once buried in Athens under a false name. Karras’s dog. My jaw clenched hard enough to crack.


“He sold routes,” Dante said. “Dock manifests. Container numbers. Not enough to burn us—but enough to test how far we’d let him reach.”


“He wanted me to find out,” I said, voice flat. “He wants war.”


Dante’s silence was agreement.


I turned from the window. “And Faith?”


He hesitated. “She doesn’t know yet. But she feels it. Everyone does. The house is holding its breath.”


Of course it was.


The villa always knew when blood was about to spill.


I poured whiskey into a glass I didn’t need, the sound too loud in the quiet room. “Find him.”


“We’ve tried,” Dante said. “His men went dark. The villa in Amalfi was emptied two days ago.”


He paused, then added quietly, “He took Paolo’s cross from the chapel.”


That stopped me.


Paolo. The youngest soldier. The one who still called me boss like it meant something holy. The one I’d ordered Matteo to put down when loyalty turned inconvenient.


He’d done it — my word, Matteo’s hand — and neither of us had slept clean since.


And now Matteo had taken the boy’s cross. Not to mourn him. To make me remember.


The glass hit the desk harder than I meant it to.


“Get everyone ready,” I said. “We burn every route he touched. Every contact. Every shipment. I want the sea clean again.”


Dante nodded once and turned for the door.


“Dante.”


“Dante.”


He stopped.


“If I hesitate,” I said, “remind me what he did.”


“I won’t need to,” he said. “You’ll feel it.”


He left me alone with the weight of my own name.


⸻


Faith found me an hour later.


She didn’t knock either.


She wore black—simple, sleeveless, the kind of black that looked like defiance instead of mourning. Her eyes searched my face the way only hers could, looking not for weakness but for truth.


“What happened?”


I didn’t lie. “Matteo sold us.”


She went still. Not shocked. Not surprised. Just… sad. “I felt it,” she said softly. “The air changed.”


She crossed the room, close enough that the scent of her skin tangled with smoke and salt. She reached up, touched my jaw like she was daring me to still be human.


“You’re shaking,” she whispered.


“I’m not.”


“You are,” she said. “Inside.”


I caught her wrist. “Don’t touch me when I’m like this.”


“Then when?” she asked. “When you’ve killed your brother? When you’ve burned what’s left of your soul?”


Her voice wasn’t raised, but it sliced through the air like a blade.


I stepped closer. “He betrayed us.”


“He betrayed you,” she corrected. “And you’ll destroy everything that isn’t him trying to get to him.”


She wasn’t wrong. That was what made it worse.


I reached for her anyway, gripping her waist hard enough that she gasped. “You think I won’t? You think I don’t know what this costs?”


Her eyes didn’t flinch. “I think you stopped counting a long time ago.”


Something in me cracked. The part that had been pretending to stay calm split wide open.


I kissed her like fury had found a mouth. The taste of her was salt and defiance and the only thing in this world that still felt alive. Her hands shoved at my chest, not to push me away but to remind me she could. Then she gave in, pulling me down, dragging my control with her.


We collided against the desk, papers scattering like birds taking flight. My hand tangled in her hair, my mouth tracing the pulse at her throat. Her breath hitched when I whispered, “You think I’m the monster they say?”


She looked up, eyes wet and fierce. “No. I think you’re worse. Because you love me in the middle of it.”


That broke the last of my restraint.


The kiss turned rough, then desperate, then reverent. Her nails bit into my shoulders, and I let the pain anchor me. This wasn’t softness. It was survival—two people trying to remember they were still flesh and not myth.


When I pulled back, her lips were swollen, her breath uneven. “If you go after him,” she said, “you’ll lose more than your brother.”


“I already did,” I said. “The moment he turned.”


She pressed her forehead to mine. “Then promise me one thing.”


“Name it.”


“Don’t let the house hear you break.”


I exhaled a sound that wasn’t quite a laugh. “Then you’ll have to break quietly with me.”


Her fingers found mine. “Always.”


By the time Dante returned, the sun had climbed just enough to make the world honest again.


He didn’t need to speak. The look on his face said everything.


“Where?” I asked.


“The chapel,” he said. “Matteo sent a message.”


My blood went cold. “Show me.”


We crossed the courtyard in silence. Guards fell into step, their faces tight, their eyes fixed anywhere but mine.


The chapel door was open. Inside, the candle that had burned since my father’s reign was gone. In its place, on the altar, lay Paolo’s cross—bent, bloodied, and tied with a strip of linen torn from one of my own shirts.


Matteo’s handwriting marked the marble beneath it:


“Every kingdom starts with a sacrifice.”


The rage that rose in me wasn’t fire—it was ice. Sharp. Precise. Final.


“Dante,” I said.


He waited.


“Burn it.”


“The chapel?”


“All of it.”


He hesitated. “Lorenzo—”


“Do it,” I said, my voice low, cold, absolute. “The dead don’t need monuments. They need vengeance.”


Faith stood behind me. She didn’t speak. She didn’t move. She just watched as men carried the first canisters of fuel through the doorway.


When the match was struck, the flames caught fast—racing up the curtains, devouring the rafters, turning memory into smoke.


The bell above the chapel cracked once before it fell.


I didn’t look away.


The firelight painted Faith’s face gold and terrible. Her hand slipped into mine, trembling but unyielding.


Matteo’s message had been clear. Mine would be clearer.


“Send word to every port,” I told Dante. “Every name Matteo ever touched. Every man he ever bribed. I want them ash by dawn.”


“And when we find him?”


I looked at the flames, at the last of Paolo’s cross melting into the altar. “Then I end this family the way he began it—bloody and unforgiving.”


Faith turned to me, tears caught in her lashes. “And when it’s done?”


“When it’s done,” I said, “we rebuild from the bones.”


The chapel collapsed inward with a sound like thunder, and for a moment the entire villa bowed its head as if in mourning.


But I didn’t mourn.


Not yet.


I watched until the last ember went out, until the smoke turned the sky the color of my brother’s sin.


Then I said the words that sealed every oath I had left:


“Find Matteo Bellucci. Bring him home.”


Dante’s voice was a whisper. “Alive?”


I looked at Faith—her trembling breath, her courage like glass—and said quietly,


“No.”

Chapter 12

The Reckoning


Faith


Fire leaves its own stench.


By morning the villa breathed smoke. Ash dusted the lemons like a penance, and the courtyard fountains ran gray until Marcella ordered the water cut because grief shouldn’t pretend to be pretty. Men moved in twos and threes; women moved in ones and watched the men. Every step sounded like it owed an apology.


The chapel was a ruin. Stone ribs. Black tooth of bell in the rubble. Heat still wavered over the altar like a ghost slow to learn it no longer had a body. I stood at the threshold and didn’t cross. The threshold was enough: the place where vows remembered themselves and then decided whether to keep going.


A chair creaked behind me. Marcella sat under the loggia with a blanket across her knees, rosary wound so tight around her fingers the beads had stuck dents into her skin. She looked older than yesterday by a decade and calmer by a century.


“You did not sleep,” she said.


“Neither did you.”


She tipped her chin toward the chapel, the way Neapolitans point with everything but their fingers. “We’ll scrub the soot. We won’t fix the story.”


“What story?”


“That he burned the only room where he forgave himself.”


She had given me rosemary for remembrance and mint for clarity. Today she gave me nothing. Perhaps some days a woman should hold her own symbols.


Dante’s footsteps cut the courtyard—no wasted rhythm, no sound he didn’t choose. He didn’t pause to look at the wreck. He came to me as if the air between us had orders.


“Three items,” he said, as if reading from an index. “First, Split’s Englishman is already in flight. He boarded with two bags and one lie. We kept the lie. Second, the port. The boxes are back on paper and in motion in fact. Ten percent agreed, a penalty he will call etiquette. Third…” His eyes flicked, just once, toward the chapel. “Marcella’s inner circle has changed shape.”


“How?” I asked.


“Two of her girls called in sick,” he said. “Sick calls don’t happen here. One of them has a cousin who cleaned the contractor armory. The cousin now answers a phone no longer in service.”


“Inside,” I said.


“Inside,” he agreed.


Marcella didn’t flinch. “I feed men. I don’t guard them. If someone took a key when I wasn’t looking, that’s my failure and my warning. I prefer it as warning.” Her eyes moved to me, not unkindly. “You feel it, yes? The wire tightening.”


“I feel it,” I said.


“Then don’t pretend to be soft,” she said. “Soft women get folded and put away.” She nodded at the soot. “Today you wear black without mourning.”


“Tell me what to do,” I said, because pride is a stupid expense.


She smiled, brief and fierce. “Be what he needs. Even if he doesn’t like needing it.”


Lorenzo found me hours later in the study—the room that had learned to smell like strategy and worn-out mercy. He had showered. The scent of smoke had rinsed from his skin, not from his eyes. He closed the door with that quiet he used when rooms had to learn to be careful.


“They’re spreading the story,” he said without prelude. “Naples. That I burned prayer because I’ve run out of grace.”


“What did you burn?” I asked.


“Witnesses,” he said. Then, almost gentle: “And excuses.”


He crossed to the desk where the map still lived under a film of ash. His fingers hovered over Adriatic lines. “Dante wants containment,” he said. “I want arithmetic.” His mouth turned. “Matteo wants applause.”


“And me?” I asked.


“You want survival,” he said. “For all of us.”


He was wrong and he was right. There are days a woman wants to live; there are days she wants the right deaths.


“Dante says inside,” I told him. “Marcella’s girls.”


“I know,” he said. “I’ll handle it.”


“You mean punish,” I said.


He looked up. “Sometimes the same thing.”


I stepped into his space because some truths deserve to be told close. “Don’t bleed the kitchen when the pier is still slick. Choose your enemies in order.”


His hand rose—hesitated—then cupped my jaw the way men do when they don’t know if they’re asking permission or giving it. “Stay with me,” he said. Not an order. A line tied between ships in weather.


“I’m not leaving,” I said.


“Then say it again when I come back.”


He didn’t kiss me. He didn’t have to. He left the room and the heat of him stayed like a vow that knew it needed witnessed.


The villa has three kinds of silence. There’s the silence of obedience (useful), the silence of fear (dangerous), and the silence of calculation (inevitable). By late afternoon, all three had settled in layers.


I walked the long hall that still remembered the knife and counted the guards by their shoulders. Rocco shadowed me like a superstition.


“Don’t argue,” he said when I looked at him.


“I wasn’t going to,” I lied.


“You were,” he said. “You always do.”


In the kitchen, women paused and then resumed—chopping, kneading, stirring. Flour turned their hands into ghosts. Marcella’s domain had not forgiven the chapel; it had decided to outlast it. She tilted her head toward the far door. I followed the tilt.


In the corridor beyond, Dante waited with a folder and a blade of a smile. “I need you to read this like a teacher,” he said.


“Like a what?”


“Like a woman who spots cheating at twenty paces.”


He opened the folder. Receipts, timetables, maintenance logs—the handwriting of men who thought ink could hide them. He tapped three signatures.


“See the tails?” he asked. “The way the loops change? That’s left-handed man trying to be right-handed. He signed in a hurry. He thought nobody would check the old pages.”


“Who?” I asked.


“Luisa’s cousin,” he said. “The girl with the sick day. And a maintenance man who owed him favors. Both men. One favor.”


“And Luisa?”


He didn’t answer because she stepped into the corridor as if the house had called her. Early twenties. Quiet mouth. Hands that did their work without complaint. She avoided my eyes politely until she didn’t.


“I didn’t know,” she said before anyone asked. “I wiped the shelf. I didn’t count the keys. The cousin said the log was fine. I believed him.”


Dante’s face had two settings: blade and mirror. He chose mirror. “Believing isn’t knowing.”


“I know now,” she said. “If you let me, I’ll help you close the door I opened.”


He looked at me. He was asking, don’t say it out loud, whether the kitchen would accept a girl who had been used, whether I knew the difference between weakness and malice.


“Keep her,” I said. “Use her. Give her harder work, not less.” To Luisa: “You don’t get to be soft again. Soft got us a corpse.”


She didn’t cry. Good. She nodded like a woman who had earned the right to hear a hard truth without flinching. Dante dismissed her with a small motion and the folder with a larger one.


“Matteo?” I asked.


“Gone to ground,” he said. “Which in his language means he’s exactly where rumor can still see him.”


“Why tell me this and not Lorenzo?” I asked.


“I told him,” Dante said. “He needed you to hear it from someone who wouldn’t make it sound like a sentence.”


“You think I forgive where he punishes,” I said.


“I think you measure what he burns,” he said. “And you keep his fires from jumping roofs.”


Footsteps. The study door. Lorenzo again, sleeves rolled, tie gone, eyes fixed on a point he hadn’t chosen but had accepted. “We have him.”


“Matteo?” I asked.


“The men he’s borrowing,” Lorenzo said. “Karras’s Croats. They think a warehouse makes them interesting.” He looked at me without looking away from the choice that had walked in with him. “You stay.”


“No,” I said, before sense could remind me it liked breathing.


He smiled without humor. “That’s not how this works.”


“You told me to look where you look,” I said. “You don’t get to shut my eyes when they’re inconvenient.”


Dante watched both of us the way a mathematician watches a proof he’s not sure he wants to be correct. Rocco watched the window, which is to say he watched the city’s temper. Somewhere inside, Marcella prayed without a chapel and made the kitchen her altar.


Lorenzo closed the distance between us and set his hand at the base of my throat, a weight, not a threat. “I need you whole.”


“I’m more whole near you,” I said. “You know that.”


He breathed once, twice, the way a man does when he’s counting something that isn’t numbers. Then he nodded as if he had just chosen a country and signed it in his own blood. “You don’t leave my shadow.”


“I don’t intend to.”


“You don’t speak unless I ask you to.”


“I don’t intend to do that either,” I said, and his mouth twitched in a way that meant the day hadn’t managed to break all of him.


We moved.


Dusk sharpened Split into something almost beautiful. The warehouse district sulked along the water as if it resented commerce but liked its money. Our car joined the slow stream of vans and small trucks pretending to be bored. The men we passed pretended harder.


Lorenzo’s hand rested on my thigh—casual, proprietary, an anchor disguised as something else. Dante murmured directions without looking down. Rocco drove like a rumor: present, deniable.


“Two on the door,” Dante said. “One back by the loading ramp. One on the roof line. The ones inside will have confidence.”


“Confidence dies fastest,” Lorenzo said.


He turned his head enough that our eyes met in the dark. The look he gave me would have been mercy in another life. Here it was permission.


“Stay behind me,” he said. “But close enough that if I fall your first step hits me, not the floor.”


“I’m not in love with the second half of that sentence,” I said.


“Neither am I,” he said. “But the math wants what it wants.”


We slid along the wall, shadows in a city that had seen too many. The back ramp’s man smoked with the concentration of the doomed. Rocco took him—no performance, no sound. Dante ghosted the roofline with a laser and a length of invisible string. The door learned to open for us.


Inside, the warehouse smelled of oil and misplaced optimism. Lights buzzed too bright for true criminals and too dim for honest commerce. Crates. Racks. A forklift that had known better days and worse nights.


Matteo’s voice arrived before he did, pitched for audience and echo. “—so you tell your Greek he can have his half back when he returns the half he thinks is his.”


“Matteo,” Lorenzo said, like a rebuke and a brother’s name.


He stepped into the aisle as if he had been waiting for a cue. A white shirt with a hoop of blood drying at the left shoulder. Smile that didn’t reach what mattered. Three Croats with attitudes they thought translated.


“Don,” he said lightly. “Dante. Faith.” A little salute, two fingers, a boy at a parade.


“Enough,” Lorenzo said.


“Agreement,” Matteo returned. “Enough pretending we’re not solving the same problem.”


“We’re not,” Lorenzo said. “I’m solving you.”


The Croats reacted, small twitch, gun-hand confidence. Dante raised nothing but his attention. Rocco became gravity with knuckles.


“You bled at my dock,” Lorenzo said to his brother. “You took Paolo’s cross. You invited Rome by lighting sound.”


“I invited fear,” Matteo said. “Your favorite currency when you remember you have it.”


“You confuse fear with applause,” Lorenzo said. “One ends with quiet. The other ends with blood.”


“Then let it end with blood,” Matteo said, and for a second I saw the boy who had stood at his father’s coffin and promised the world would pay with interest.


Silence. Crates. The sea muttering to concrete. The math balancing on a knife.


I stepped forward into Lorenzo’s shadow because that was the agreement and because I needed him to feel me there. His hand found mine without looking. He squeezed once. Don’t speak. Don’t move. Be my proof.


The nearest Croat tried to be brave and found arrogance instead. “Enough family drama,” he said in Romanian-tinted English. “We take the woman. You talk like men.”


Rocco’s laugh was low and brief. Dante’s mouth didn’t move. Lorenzo didn’t turn his head, but every molecule in the room learned who owned air.


“No,” he said.


Guns make two sounds when rooms are honest: the small click of decision and the larger cough of regret. The Croat reached decision. Regret didn’t arrive because Dante shot him in the foot before the rest of his body understood what had been decided.


Everything that could have become noise paused.


Matteo’s grin cracked. “Still fast, brother.”


“Fast is mercy,” Lorenzo said. “Slow is teaching.”


Matteo looked at me then—not sexual, not even strategic—like a man checking if a mirror still worked. “You think he can be saved by love?” he asked.


“I think he can be aimed,” I said.


His eyes softened—an old, stupid tenderness that meant once, long ago, he had liked me because I had made Lorenzo laugh. “And if you have to choose?” he asked, too softly.


“I already did,” I said.


He did not enjoy hearing it. The grin returned—harder, worse. “Then let’s finish Sunday school.”


He flicked his fingers, a cue he thought was subtle. Two men from the far aisle tried to be theater. Rocco handled one with an education. Dante tamed the other with anatomy. The air changed temperature. The warehouse chose a side: ours.


Matteo looked at the math, finally. “You didn’t come to talk,” he said.


“I came to take what’s mine,” Lorenzo said.


“Me.” Half-laugh. “Then take me.”


Lorenzo didn’t move his gun. He didn’t need to. “Kneel.”


We were past theater now. We were past brothers. We were in the place where houses decide whether they keep breathing.


Matteo stared at the floor. Then he sank, slow, a man who liked to pretend he chose his fall. He looked up. The grin was gone. The boy was back for one white second.


“Make it clean,” he said.


“No,” Lorenzo said. His voice didn’t break. It bled. “I make it true.”


He lowered the gun.


The door at the far end banged open. New footsteps. Not Croats. Not ours. A woman’s voice, sharp with an order that had learned to be obeyed: “Stop.”


Marcella.


She walked into the aisle like a verdict wrapped in black. No rosary. No kitchen steam. Only eyes that had decided which son got to be saved and which memory got to keep a chair.


“Not here,” she said to Lorenzo. “Not like this.”


He didn’t look at her. He looked at me. His mouth was a line I knew how to read. If I listen to her, it is because you made me able to.


“Stand,” he told Matteo instead.


Matteo stood. Swayed. Smiled because that’s how he stitched his skin back together.


Marcella’s gaze cut him. “You come home,” she said. “You make confession with your hands, not your mouth. You carry the stones. You close the doors.” To Lorenzo: “And you decide his punishment where the house can witness and survive it.”


“Rome will witness,” Dante warned.


“Then we teach Rome to watch,” Marcella said. “And to learn nothing.”


The city outside changed color, as if sunset had heard its cue. Lorenzo lowered the gun—not mercy; mathematics.


“Rocco,” he said. “Take my brother home.”


Rocco moved. Matteo didn’t resist. Marcella stepped aside like a queen who remembered where to stand so nobody forgot God was watching.


We left the Croats to their mistakes and the warehouse to its echo. In the car, Lorenzo’s hand closed over my knee so hard my breath hitched. He didn’t apologize. He didn’t loosen. He needed the proof of bone.


“Say it,” he said, voice raw silk.


“I’m here,” I told him.


“Again.”


“I’m here.”


“Again.”


“I’m here,” I said, until the word became a rope and we both held.


Night turned the villa back into a fortress. The kitchen ran without flourishes; the guards counted without speaking. The chapel ruin cooled to memory. In the study, Dante laid out a new map: not ports and routes this time—rooms. The kitchen. The contractor locker. The chapel. The guard post at the east stairs where a logbook had learned to lie.


“Inside,” he said. “We close first. Then we look out.”


Marcella stood in the door and didn’t cross. “He sleeps in the north room,” she said. “Not his.” She looked at Lorenzo. “He doesn’t get old rights.”


“Tomorrow,” Lorenzo said. “We speak in front of the house.”


“And tonight?” she asked.


He looked at me. The question wasn’t about punishment. It was about whether the man who loved me could still aim the man who ruled them.


“Tonight we count,” I said. “Names, keys, passes, debts. And we cut everything that doesn’t balance.”


He nodded once and the room obeyed, as if the verb had been waiting for a subject.


When we were finally alone, heat pooled under the skin of the night. Lorenzo reached for me and I went because I already was. His kiss wasn’t gentle; it was deliberate. A claiming without cruelty, a brand without fire. He lifted me, set me on the edge of the desk where maps had learned our weight, and pressed his mouth to the hollow of my throat like a man remembering the way home.


“Say it,” he murmured into my skin, softer now.


“I’m yours,” I answered, not as surrender, but as calculus: the one equation I wanted to keep solving.


He held me there—hard, present, breathing me like oxygen—and the room stopped shaking.


Outside, the sea kept moving. Inside, the house decided to survive. Tomorrow would bruise. Tomorrow would cut. Tomorrow we would stand a brother up in front of the family and decide how a kingdom keeps its crown.


Tonight we made sure the crown remembered why it wanted to. And for the first time since the fire, my body believed the morning might come.

Chapter 13

Lace and Daggers


Lorenzo


A meeting was called.


Word went out at dusk and came back as bodies—soldiers in clean shirts, staff with tight mouths, women whose prayers were working hands. They filled the courtyard in ranked silence, eyes turned toward the portico where my father had once broken a man with a sentence and then ordered more wine.


The chapel was a smoldering jawbone behind us. The ruin made a stage. Good. Let the stone remember.


Rocco brought Matteo through the gate just as the bells in the fishing village to the south rang their seventh hour. Two guards flanked him, one step behind, like parentheses around a phrase that could still change the meaning of the sentence. He wore a fresh shirt, sleeve cut to leave the bandage free, and the smile of a boy who’d drawn blood and wanted applause for the artwork.


It died when he saw the crowd. Even his kind of courage respects math.


Dante stood to my right, a ledger in his hand like a liturgy. Marcella took the front row with the women of the kitchen, black still on her like an answer. Faith stood at the edge of the portico, a straight line in a sea of curves, near enough that I could feel the angle of her attention and the heat of her breath when she forgot to be careful. I didn’t look at her. The house would read it as permission to hope. Tonight wasn’t for hope.


“Matteo Bellucci,” I said.


The name moved through the courtyard like a tide: out, back, taking loose things with it.


“You brought Karras into my water. You sold numbers that aren’t yours. You took Paolo’s cross.”


He tilted his head, the old mischief flickering. “I brought fear back to your name.”


“You brought Rome to my door,” Dante said, dry as paper. “There’s a difference.”


Matteo shrugged. “Rome already wants our lives. I gave them a show. We used to be good at those.”


He looked at the men and got the wrong kind of silence in reply. Even his heart heard it. He turned to me again, and something old—older than Athens, older than the docks—passed through his eyes. Shame tastes the same in boys and men.


“You want me to kneel?” he asked. “Here? In front of the women and the maids and the men who keep getting paid late?”


I let him hear nothing from me but air. He tried another door.


“She has you soft,” he said, flicking his chin toward Faith without looking. “Or precise. I can’t tell which anymore.”


Precision is a blade; softness is a shroud. He wanted me to choose the wrong one out loud.


“Confess,” I said.


“And if I don’t?”


My gun stayed holstered. My voice did the killing. “Then I decide your sentence without hearing your truth. And when I do that, I don’t give back anything I take.”


He swallowed. He had always hated not controlling the room.


He looked out over the house—over men who’d bled for us and women who’d buried the ones who bled too much—and chose cowardice’s clever cousin: performance.


“I did it,” he said, louder than necessary. “I moved the numbers. I used Greeks who pretend to be clean. I made the corridor louder so the city would remember who we are. Because this”—he cut his hand toward the ruined chapel—“is what happens when we forget. We pray. We get polite. We turn our knives into candles. And then a boy dies for it.”


Paolo’s name broke the skin of the evening. There were sounds from the crowd—small, sharp, private.


I stepped forward. “Paolo died because men wanted credit more than shelter. He died because you raised your voice where I wanted silence. He died because you would rather be worshiped than obeyed.”


Matteo sneered. “Obey or die. That the family motto now?”


“It always was,” Marcella said from the front, calm and terrible. “You only liked it better when your father said it to someone else.”


He flinched. He recovered. He always did.


“I brought something back,” Dante said, lifting a small evidence bag by one corner like a sin. A metal key on an orange contractor tag. The number embossed was cheap and municipal. “Maintenance locker at the east armory. Luisa’s cousin signed it. He no longer answers breathing.”


A low ripple in the crowd. Not surprise. Inventory.


Matteo kept his gaze on me. “You going to shoot me here, in front of the altar?”


“No,” I said.


I let the word live there, one beat, two. I could feel Faith watching my mouth like it was the only weapon I had left that mattered.


“You’re going to work,” I continued. “And the house is going to watch.”


He laughed. “What? You want penitence? You want me to carry stone like a monk and recite schedules until God forgives my handwriting?”


“I want you to learn the difference between fear and power,” I said. “And men like you only learn with your hands.”


I turned to Rocco. “North wing. Empty room. Window bars. Two guards. No liquor. No women. No phone.” To the men: “Six months. Every morning he works. Every night he eats after the kitchen and before the dogs. He fixes what he broke. He rebuilds the chapel with his hands. He scrubs the corridors he opened. He carries water to the kitchen and wood to the oven and stone to the altar.”


Someone in the back—one of the young ones who still thought declarations were a personality—couldn’t help himself. “Six months? That’s it?”


I didn’t look at him. “He fails once, he loses a finger. He fails twice, he loses a hand. He fails three times—” I finally met his eyes “—and I give him the clean death he asked for in the warehouse.”


The yard found a new silence. Not obedience, not fear. Calculation. This silence would save us if we kept it.


Matteo stared at me as if I had spit in his food and he had to decide whether hunger was stronger than pride. “Prison,” he said.


“Debt,” I said. “You pay it in labor. You pay it where the house can witness, and survive your payment.”


He flicked his gaze toward Faith, quick and mean. “And when your proof gets bored of martyrdom and decides she liked men who painted cities better?”


The wrong men murmured. Good. Let them reveal themselves. Dante made a mark in his head.


I felt the weight of the courtyard tip toward heat. I let it. I let them think they had almost seen me lose it. I stepped down into the yard—as close to him as I used to stand when we were boys and our father’s belt had found us both on the same morning for different sins.


“You will not say her name,” I whispered. Only he heard it. “You will not look for her eyes in rooms where you’re meant to look at floors. You will not test me with your tongue. You will put your back into stone, your knees into stairs, and your blood into work. If you do anything else, I will erase you from Naples with a sentence and let the city think mercy died with you.”


He breathed hard once. Twice. He looked at the ruin. He looked at our mother. He looked at the men who’d roll dice later to decide whether they agreed with me. Then he did the only thing he could do that didn’t end in a body on warm stone.


He nodded.


“Good,” Marcella said, and the crowd released the breath they’d been holding. “Rocco, take my son.”


They led him out to a chorus of small, necessary noises: the scrape of chairs, the creak of leather, the click of safe weapons resetting to safe tempers. The house had its verdict. It also had its spectacle. Both are useful.


When they were gone, I stayed where I was until the last man left the yard. The women dispersed first, which is how you know a kingdom isn’t falling—women go early to get the work set for morning. Dante waited. Faith waited. Marcella did not. She vanished into the dark like a plan that wanted quiet.


“You chose life,” Dante said.


“For the house,” I said.


“And for him?”


I looked toward the north wing where a barred window had just become a metronome. “For the house,” I said again, and he didn’t make me repeat it a third time.


He closed the ledger, the snap of cardboard small and fatal. “Karras is already buying a new liaison. The Englishman called a priest he doesn’t believe in and a lawyer he does. We have maybe forty-eight hours of quiet.”


“Then let’s spend them,” I said. “Keys, passes, routes. Flush everything that smells like worship of the wrong altar.”


He nodded and moved. He was always best when the verbs were clear.


I finally looked at Faith.


She hadn’t come down from the portico. A gust had lifted hair at her temple and left it there like a careless blessing. The courtyard lamps put gold at her throat, and the shadows put knives along her cheekbones. She said nothing. She didn’t have to. I could read the page I had just handed her.


“You wanted blood,” I said.


“I wanted your soul,” she answered. “Sometimes those are the same thing.”


“Not tonight.”


“No,” she said softly. “Not tonight.”


I reached for her and then didn’t, because there was a hierarchy to the order of things. The house first. My brother’s debt second. The woman who kept me precise third. That was the lie I recited to remain the man they needed. The truth was simpler and more dangerous.


“Come,” I said, and the word was only for her.


We crossed through corridors that had remembered how to breathe now that a verdict lived in their walls. At the study door she pressed her palm to the wood like it had a pulse and she was checking whether the house still planned to live. Inside, the map waited. I didn’t touch it.


“You kept him alive,” she said when the latch clicked behind us. “You also took his crown.”


“I took his audience,” I said. “He’ll learn to work without it or he’ll die regretting he never learned to work at all.”


She came close then, the way storms do when the sky decides to be honest. “And you?”


“I’ll earn you a morning,” I said. “One. Then I’ll try for two.”


Her hand found my chest—flat, warm, human. “You could have ended him. You didn’t.”


“I ended the part of him that keeps burning other people to stay warm,” I said. “If that’s not enough, I’ll finish the rest when the city isn’t looking.”


She nodded, a small, fierce thing. The air between us thickened with all the ways men and women find to remember they are still alive. I let my forehead touch hers, a private truce after public judgment.


“Say it,” I murmured.


“I’m here,” she said.


“And you’re mine.”


“I’m yours,” she breathed, not as surrender, but as a woman who’s loyalty is to her man.


The heat climbed. I let it. Then I banked it with a palm at her jaw and a kiss that promised the rest of it later, when there weren’t names to cut and routes to reseal.


I pulled back first. “Tomorrow the house sees him carry stone. I want you to stand with Marcella and watch.”


“Because the men will be looking for a reason to believe the sentence wasn’t mercy.”


“Because I want them to know what I call strength,” I said. “It looks like work. It looks like a woman not flinching.”


Her mouth tilted. “I don’t flinch.”


“I know,” I said. “That’s why I use you.”


She smiled the kind of smile that makes a man remember he still wants to live long enough to see it again. Then she sobered.


“There’s something else,” she said. “Dante told me two of the guards in the east hall still say I make you soft. They’re new. They’re stupid. But stupidity is contagious when men are scared.”


“I’ll cut it out,” I said.


“No,” she answered. “You’ll let me.”


The refusal shocked a laugh out of me—hoarse, brief, a human sound in a room that had forgotten them. “How?”


“Let me run the morning,” she said. “Breakfast. Orders. The schedule that makes everything look inevitable. I want everyone to see that the Don’s woman knows the house by the ribs and the rhythm.”


“You want them to obey you,” I said.


“I want them to obey you harder because they had to pass through me,” she said. “You call that soft. Dante calls it infiltration. Marcella calls it housekeeping. I call it survival.”


I looked at her the way men look at the sea when they decide to live with its moods. “Do it,” I said.


She nodded once and slipped away with the light. I stood alone for the first time that day with nothing but maps and choices. My hands were steady. The window held my reflection without judgment. Out beyond the cliff, a ship cut south, its wake a white line that dissolved to nothing—a moving proof that even scars fade if no one keeps touching them.


Dante returned at midnight with a list: names from the kitchen to the quay, keys changed, doors doubled, routes shifted. I initialed lines that would cost me friends and money and made a mark beside one that would cost me sleep.


“The Englishman?” I asked.


“He’ll try to replace himself in Split before dawn,” Dante said. “I have a man there who doesn’t like priests or lawyers.”


“We don’t need a headline,” I said. “We need a pause.”


He understood. He always did. “I’ll make it look like he left his courage in a bar.”


“Good.” I hesitated. “Marcella’s girls?”


“Luisa stays,” he said. “The cousin doesn’t. The rest got the story and the schedule. Fear will do the teaching.”


“Fear is loud,” I said. “Make sure the ledger stays quiet.”


He smiled like a knife back in a drawer. “We’re learning.”


When he left, I finally sat. Not because I was tired, but because I had to practice the thing I had chosen to preach patience. Power is timing. Mercy is strategy. Love is a risk ledger you learn how to carry or you stop pretending to run a house that wants to survive you.


In the north wing, iron slid into slots. A lock learned a rhythm. Somewhere in that room Matteo stared at the dark and told himself stories in which he was still the hero. In the kitchen, a girl who had almost broken us laid out coffee cups for morning—the exact number we’d need, no more, no less. In our bed, Faith would not sleep. She would listen to the sea and decide which parts of me needed sharpening and which needed saving.


I looked toward what remained of the chapel and let the ruin preach: stone holds heat a long time, but it holds shape longer. We would build again. The altar would rise from the ash. A cross would return to the wall when we earned it.


Until then, the house would have to learn a different prayer.


Work.


I picked up the pen, signed the orders that made the night honest, and closed the book.


Tomorrow, my brother would carry stone.


Tomorrow, I would carry the crown.


And if either task failed, Naples would learn there are still sentences in this house that end with one word.

Chapter 14

The Fire Between Us


Lorenzo


The house learned a new rhythm overnight.


Hammers at dawn. Stone lifted in low, grunting measures. The scrape of wheelbarrows across the courtyard flagstones where the chapel once kept us honest. Men didn’t speak much. When they did, it was in the short sentences of labor and obedience.


Through the study windows I watched Matteo shoulder rock to the ruin and back; watched Rocco pace beside him with the patient cruelty of a metronome. No liquor. No laughter. No audience but the family he’d tried to set on fire. And me.


Naples loves a spectacle. I took that from him.


Dante entered on the second hour without knocking—shirt crisp, jaw dark with the kind of stubble a man keeps when sleep has lost the negotiation. He set down a thin folder and a thicker one.


“The thin one is what we can do,” he said. “The thick one is what we must.”


“What we can,” I repeated.


“Split is quiet,” he said. “Our Englishman found religion—left town before his confession could be taken. Patras is skittish but transactional. Vassilis will take our oxygen and call it grace. If we keep routes tight for forty-eight hours, we teach the sea to forget Matteo’s name.”


“And what we must.”


“Inside,” he said simply. “Keys, passes, the morning room ledger. The two guards who called Faith your soft spot are gone from the east stairs—‘transferred’ to a contract in Taranto that involves counting fish hooks until they learn arithmetic. Luisa stays. She asked for triple duty. I gave it to her.”


He watched me the way he watches an approaching army. “You’ll want to see breakfast.”


“Why?”


“Because Faith ran it like a parade and a sermon,” he said. “Everyone came away believing something—some that you’re stronger, others that she is. Either way, the math works.”


I didn’t answer. I was already picturing her at the head of the table—dark dress, sleeves rolled, the morning steam rising around her like incense. Speaking quietly, pointing precisely. Bending the room without raising her voice. There are a dozen ways to hold a kingdom; hers is a hand on the pulse.


I went to the balcony. Below, Matteo lifted another stone. Sweat darkened his shirt between the blades and down the spine—humbling lines. He didn’t look up. He knew I was there.


“Tell me if he stumbles,” I said.


“I’ll tell you if he learns,” Dante replied. He took a breath like a man about to light a fuse. “One more thing. Karras sent a test last night—three men to the outer road. They didn’t make it to the gate.”


“Leave them where they fell,” I said. “The city needs to smell what it’s asking for.”


Dante nodded and left the way he came. The folder stayed. The morning shone cold and clean, as if the sky had decided to be new and would punish anyone who didn’t behave like it.


By the third hour Faith stepped into the study—no knock, a woman who’d earned the right to be part of the air. A smudge of flour kissed one wrist where she’d tied back the sleeves. Her hair was pinned high; one curl had mutinied and grazed her jaw. She smelled faintly of lemons and steel.


“The kitchen is a clock again,” she said.


“And the men?” I asked.


“They think clocks run themselves,” she said. “I let them keep that innocence.”


Her gaze moved past me to the balcony, to the courtyard where my brother worked. “He hasn’t begged.”


“He won’t,” I said. “Begging is an audience. I took that too.”


Her eyes came back to mine—clear, unflinching. “You’re shaking.”


I looked at my hands. I wasn’t. Not on the outside. She meant the place a man keeps his tremors. The place only one person gets to steady.


“Come with me,” I said.


We walked the long corridor—two guards ahead, two behind—past the chapel’s black ribs to the north wing. The door to the empty room stood open. A bed, a table, a basin, a barred window. No drink. No phone. A life measured by labor and quiet.


Rocco straightened. “He’s holding the line,” he said. “Back and forth. No talk.”


“Good,” I said, and waved him out.


I stepped to the threshold and looked at my brother.


“Water,” I told him.


He carried the bucket without looking at my face, without slopping a drop, without letting the tremor in his arms show as anything other than the mathematics of what a body weighs when a man puts his name back into it. He set the bucket down like a penitent offering, still not meeting my eyes.


“The stone by noon,” I said. “The corridor by dusk.”


He nodded. No grin. No insult. Respect? No. Calculation. He’s learning what has value.


I turned away and felt his stare not at my back but somewhere lower, older—like boys in a church trying to guess where the power is kept. I left him the room and its window and the first afternoon of a sentence I intend to enforce until the house forgives itself.


In the corridor, Faith waited with a stillness that made the guards fidget. She didn’t speak. She didn’t touch me. She just gave me the one thing a man like me never gets in public: a place to put down the crown without dropping it.


“Come,” she said, and this time the word belonged to both of us.


We didn’t go to the bedroom. We went to the map room, because that’s where I’m most myself: lines, routes, names that pay or bleed. She closed the door—soft, precise—and leaned her back against it like she had decided to keep the rest of the world outside with her palms.


“I ran your house this morning,” she said, voice low. “It obeyed.”


“Because of you or because of me?” I asked.


“Yes,” she said. The smallest smile. Then, softer: “Breathe.”


I hadn’t realized I’d been holding it.


She crossed the floor. The lamplight painted her collarbone in a blade of gold. I watched the rise and fall of her breathing and felt my own find it. When her palms settled against my chest, heat moved through me like a command I wanted to obey.


“You haven’t eaten,” she said.


“Later.”


“You haven’t slept.”


“Later.”


“You haven’t touched me,” she said, and the room became a bell someone had struck without using their hands.


I took her wrist and laid it over the hammering at my sternum. “I am touching you.”


“I want more.”

The house was noise again outside the walls—boots, orders, stone and wheel—but in here everything narrowed to the line between her mouth and mine. And if anything tried to break the rhythm we had bled to earn, Naples would learn—again—that I still knew the shortest word a kingdom fears.


Enough.

Trust no one.

The Weight of the Crown


Faith


The rain didn’t fall—it punished. It came in sheets off the sea, hurling itself against the windows so hard it sounded like applause from ghosts. Every strike against the glass carried the same accusation: He’s still paying for what you both did.


The courtyard was empty now. The last of the workers had gone before dawn, their boots leaving muddy prints in the marble before the rain swallowed them. The scent of limestone dust still hung in the air—sharp, raw, alive. Each time I closed my eyes, I saw Matteo’s silhouette at the chapel’s broken arch, bare hands bleeding as he set another stone in place.


Matteo wasn’t dead. Not yet. Lorenzo had made sure of that.


“Let him rebuild what he desecrated,” he’d said. “Let his penance last longer than mercy ever would.”


So Matteo worked under guard, day and night, beneath the cross he’d once stained with betrayal. No comfort. No contact. No absolution. Just silence, labor, and rain.


And Lorenzo carried the weight of that decision like armor.


I moved barefoot through the halls, listening to the storm slide over the roof like a warning. The air smelled of sea and smoke and men who’d learned to whisper their fear. Servants didn’t meet my eyes anymore; their silence wasn’t loyalty—it was survival.


When I reached the study, he was exactly where I expected him. Same chair. Same untouched whiskey. Same war in his eyes. The lamp cast half his face in gold and left the other swallowed in shadow. His jacket hung open, shirt wrinkled, cuffs undone. He looked like a man who’d forgotten where his power ended and his guilt began.


“You should eat,” I said softly.


He didn’t look up. “And you should sleep.”


“I tried,” I said. “But the sea’s louder than forgiveness tonight.”


That made him glance at me—slowly, deliberately. The kind of look that could cut through a room and find the truth hiding in it. “Forgiveness isn’t part of this,” he said.


“No,” I whispered. “But punishment is.”


He exhaled, the sound rough, dangerous. “You think I enjoy watching him break?”


“I think you need to,” I said. “Because if you stop, you’ll feel what you did to him.”


He pushed back from the desk, standing. “My own blood brother betrayed me, Faith.”


“I know.”


“He chose outsiders over blood. He made deals that could’ve destroyed everything we built.”


“And you chose to make him live with it,” I said. “That’s worse than killing him.”


He stopped moving. The truth hit him like it had been waiting for the silence.


“Death is clean in your world,” I continued. “You made it dirty. You made it forever.”


His jaw tightened. “You sound like Marcella.”


“I sound like someone who still believes you have a soul.”


Something flickered behind his eyes. Not softness—something older. Worn. The look a man wears when the only way to love is to destroy a little less each time.


He came closer until the edge of the desk touched my hip. The storm outside rolled deep, rumbling through the glass like the city itself was listening.


“You think you can carry this with me?” he asked.


“I already am.”


He studied me a long moment. Then his voice dropped. “You should hate me for what I’ve done.”


“Maybe I do,” I said. “Being dragged into your world isn’t easy But I hate what Matteo did to your family.”


The air between us tightened. Something in him cracked—quietly, invisibly. His hand rose, rough palm brushing my cheek, thumb tracing the edge of my mouth like it was a confession.


“You don’t understand what this costs,” he murmured. “Making your own brother into a warning…”


“I understand enough,” I whispered. “You’re not punishing him anymore. You’re punishing yourself.”


He laughed once, a hollow sound that broke too soon. “And you’re still here.”


“Where else would I go?”


He looked at me like he wanted to believe that was a choice. “You’ll drown in me, Faith.”


“I can swim,” I said.


And that was all it took.


His hand fisted in my hair, pulling me into his kiss—fierce, hungry, devastating. The desk dug into my hip as his body pressed me back, his mouth devouring the words I might’ve said. His touch was desperate, not cruel; it trembled like penance. His lips tasted of whiskey and sleepless nights, of command and confession all tangled together.


He lifted me onto the desk, the ledgers scattering, pages fluttering like frightened birds. The storm outside answered with thunder as his mouth found my throat. I arched against him, every breath a surrender.


“I will always be here for you,” I whispered.


“You’re the only thing that makes me forget how cruel this world is.”


“Then stop remembering.”


“I can’t.” His voice broke against my skin. “That’s what keeps me alive.”


“Then let me make you forget.”


He did. With every movement, every gasp, every wordless promise, he forgot the world—until all that existed was the sound of rain, the shatter of breath, the taste of sin and survival.


When it ended, we stayed tangled in silence. My head rested against his shoulder, his heart a drumbeat under my palm. The storm had eased, but inside him, it hadn’t.


“This is the only peace I know,” he murmured.


“Then find another kind,” I said.


He laughed softly, no humor in it. “You sound like her again.”


“Marcella?”


“She still believes in saints,” he said. “I only believe in walls.”


“Then start with the one you built between you and Matteo,” I said.


His eyes met mine, unreadable. “He earns his way back, or he dies trying.”


“Then don’t look away when he does,” I whispered.


By dawn, the courtyard was slick with rain. I stood under the archway, watching the guards lead Matteo toward the chapel. He didn’t look at me, though I knew he felt my stare. His face was gaunt, his hair damp, his hands raw from work.


He carried no weapon—just a hammer and a prayer he no longer believed in.


The chapel walls were rising slowly, stone by stone, rebuilt with penance instead of mortar. Every swing of the hammer was another confession Lorenzo would never make.


Marcella appeared beside me, her shawl drawn tight around her shoulders. “He’ll finish before Easter,” she said.


“And then?” I asked.


“Then your husband decides whether forgiveness lives here,” she said. “Or whether he buries it.”


She looked at me then—eyes sharp, knowing. “Be careful, Faith. The crown doesn’t always stay on the head that wears it.”


I didn’t answer. I only watched Matteo lift another stone, his muscles straining against the weight, the sea raging behind him.


And I realized the truth Marcella hadn’t said: Forgiveness was never the point. Control was.


By afternoon, Rome’s inspectors arrived—gray suits, colder eyes. They circled the villa like vultures, asking questions disguised as courtesies. Lorenzo stood beside Dante in the dining hall, unreadable. The men flinched under his calm the way children flinch from a still flame.


“Mr. Bellucci,” one said, “your brother’s… reconstruction project. Is it penance or punishment?”


Lorenzo smiled, the kind that burned without heat. “Is there a difference?”


The man cleared his throat. “We’ll need to see it.”


“Then bring a rosary,” Lorenzo said. “You’ll need it.”


When they finally left, Dante looked drained. “You bought us a week,” he said.


“I don’t buy time,” Lorenzo murmured. “I build it.” His eyes found mine across the room. I didn’t look away.


That night, I found him on the balcony, rain misting his hair, hands gripping the rail like he was trying to hold back the sea itself.


“Was it worth it?” I asked.


He didn’t turn. “Every choice costs something.”


“And Matteo?”


“He’s paying,” he said. “So am I.”


I stepped closer, sliding my arms around his waist, pressing my cheek against his back. “You don’t have to do this alone.”


His hand found mine, rough fingers tightening. “That’s the problem, Faith. I was never meant to let anyone this close.”


“Too late,” I whispered.


He turned then, sudden, desperate. His mouth found mine in the rain—hard, claiming, full of everything we couldn’t say. The storm blurred the world beyond us. When he finally pulled back, his voice was a rasp against my ear. “Tomorrow we go to the docks. Dante says the Greeks want a meeting.”


“And tonight?”


He smiled faintly. “Tonight, we remind them who the Don belongs to.”


He lifted me, carried me inside, the thunder following us through the open doors. The sea raged on, endless and unforgiving.


And somewhere between lightning and breath, I realized the truth of what Marcella said.


The crown wasn’t his anymore. It was ours. And it was heavier than either of us knew.

Below us, the city lights shimmered through the haze. Bells rang somewhere distant, the sound soft and defiant.


Naples was still standing. So were we.


But in the ashes of that night, something new began to breathe.


Not peace.


Not victory.


A crown.


The Bellucci Legacy would live on—scarred, unholy, and unforgettable.

When I touched his arm, he didn’t flinch. He just said quietly, “We won, Faith. But look what it cost.”

“Not the Navigatore,” I said.


“No,” he murmured. “Something worse.”


He turned to me then, the shadows in his eyes too deep for daylight to touch. “They’ll come again. Rome, Athens, men who think crowns are for taking. But this time—”

His mouth curved, faint and dangerous. “To us.”


Below, the sea whispered its approval, dragging the tide higher along the rocks.


And somewhere in the city, a bell rang—not mourning, not warning—just a promise.


I looked out over the horizon, the ashes of last night glittering in the dawn.


We had burned for survival.


Now we would rise for power.


And when Naples bowed again, it wouldn’t be to the Don alone.


It would be to his queen.

Chapter 2

Cracks in the Family


Faith


Morning came with coffee that tasted like steel and a silence that tasted like secrets.


The villa was alive, but not in the way houses usually were. Its pulse beat through the footsteps of men who wouldn’t meet one another’s eyes, through the careful clink of china that tried not to sound like breaking glass, through the subtle tightening of shoulders each time two brothers occupied the same room.


I sat at the long dining table because Lorenzo had told me to, and no one else would have dared to put me there. Marcella sat across from me, rosary hidden in her hand, face calm as marble. Dante was at Lorenzo’s right, neat as a blade that knew it didn’t need to be unsheathed to be deadly. Matteo dropped into the seat opposite him, loose-limbed, grinning, already dangerous before the first bite of food.


“Principessa,” Matteo drawled, raising his glass to me. “Did you sleep well in our Don’s bed? Or was he too busy running Naples to give you proper attention?”


The words landed like sparks in dry grass. Lorenzo’s fork stilled, silver paused against porcelain. His gaze cut across the table—not loud, not violent, just a glance that felt like a trigger cocked.


“Matteo,” Dante warned quietly.


“What?” Matteo shrugged, a smirk curling his mouth. “We should celebrate. He finally brought her to the table. Surely we were allowed to talk about what was already public.”


His eyes slid to me, lingered too long. I hated that heat touched my skin anyway—unwelcome, humiliating.


“Stop testing her,” Lorenzo said, voice quiet, dangerous. “Or you’ll learn I have less patience with family than I do with strangers.”


The tension was a live wire between them, one I could almost see. Marcella didn’t flinch, but her thumb moved faster over her hidden beads. The rest of breakfast passed in silence so thick I chewed around it. By the time the plates were cleared, I knew three things for certain: Matteo was reckless, Dante was the buffer, and Lorenzo was the fuse waiting to be lit.


By afternoon, I wanted air. I told myself I was going to the terrace, but Matteo found me first. He leaned against the doorframe like a man who had never learned to ask permission.


“Come with me,” he said.


“Lorenzo—”


“Wouldn’t know. Or maybe he would.” His grin was sharp. “Let him be jealous. Jealous men make mistakes, and I want to see if my brother bleed.”


I should have refused. Every instinct screamed that this was bait, that Matteo wanted to see if he could move me across a line just to prove he could. But something darker in me—the part that wanted to know how fragile these bonds really were—went with him.


The city greeted us louder without Lorenzo’s shadow. Matteo drove like he lived: fast, swerving, laughing at rules. He took me down streets where vendors shouted curses and blessings in the same breath, through alleys where children scattered like sparrows, past shrines plastered with saints and spray paint.


“See?” he said, sweeping a hand over Naples like a conductor. “This was life. Not marble halls and quiet murders. This was what our family ruled, and my brother thought he owned it all because he could stare hard enough.”


“You didn’t agree?” I asked.


“I agreed that Lorenzo needed someone to remind him the world didn’t bow to silence. It bowed to fire.” His grin widened. “And I liked fire.”


He took me to a bar on the edge of the docks. Men glanced up, recognized him, and looked away. He bought me wine I didn’t want, told stories too loud, made jokes that burned too sharp. And through it all, I felt the danger coiling tighter. He wasn’t just reckless. He was hungry—for power, for attention, maybe for me.


When he dropped me back at the villa, he leaned close enough that I felt his breath against my ear.


“Careful, principessa. If you fell too hard for my brother, you’d never see the knife coming.”


He left me shaking—not with attraction, but with the knowledge that he meant every word. Lorenzo was waiting. Not in the hall. Not in the dining room. In the study, sleeves rolled, tie discarded, eyes black with fury he hadn’t yet decided where to place.


“Where were you?” he asked.


“With Matteo,” I admitted.


The air split. His chair scraped back, wood protesting. In two strides he was in front of me, hands braced against the desk behind me, trapping me between his arms. His face was a storm I couldn’t look away from.


“He touched you?” His voice was low, dangerous.


“No.”


“Did he look at you?”


“Yes.”


His jaw tightened. “He thought he could use you to test me. He forgot who wrote the rules in this house.”


“Lorenzo—”


He slammed his palm against the desk. Not near me. Not touching me. But the sound reverberated through my bones. “You don’t go with him again,” he growled. “Not unless you want me to put him in the ground.”


Marcella’s voice cut through the tension like incense. “Enough.” She stood in the doorway, eyes calm, hands folded. “Brothers kill enemies. Brothers forgive one another. Do not make me bury two sons in the same week.”


For a moment, the storm stilled. Lorenzo stepped back, but his gaze never left me. Later, when the house was quiet, he came to our room—not as the Don, but as a man stripped raw by fury and fear.


His kiss was savage, not tender. His hands gripped like chains, like he needed proof I hadn’t slipped away into Matteo’s orbit. I gave it back to him, matching his hunger, letting the violence turn into heat because I knew he wouldn’t break me—he’d only brand me deeper.


He slammed his mouth against mine again, crushing the air from my lungs. I tasted fury, fear, and a burning desperation on his tongue. He dragged his hands down my back, ripping the thin silk of my nightgown with a sound like tearing paper. He hauled me against the wall, chest to chest, pinning my arms above my head. "Say his name," Lorenzo growled, his dark eyes demanding. "Say you hate the way he looked at you."


"I hate it," I lied, or maybe didn't, the lie lost in the thrill of his dangerous control.


He took me like he was erasing Matteo from my skin, like he was writing his name on every inch of me. And I let him, because the truth was simpler than I wanted to admit: I craved this man even when I feared him. Especially when I feared him.


He didn't ask. He simply parted my legs with a powerful shove of his hip and drove into me with one brutal, decisive stroke. "MINE," the word was a ragged explosion against my ear, a primal roar of possession. He began to thrust with a punishing rhythm, deep and fast, using the pain and pleasure to scour away every shadow of another man. My nails raked his shoulders, demanding more, demanding the complete, absolute destruction of every thought that wasn't him.


Afterward, he held me so tightly it hurt, his breath ragged against my neck. "You’re mine," he whispered. "Not his. Never my brother’s."


"I know," I breathed back. "I chose you."


The silence after was the loudest thing in the world. When he finally left, I lay awake and stared at the ceiling. The villa creaked around me, unsettled. Matteo was reckless. Dante was controlled. Lorenzo was both fire and blade. And I was in the center of a family that might tear itself apart while the world watched. I closed my eyes and understood something I hadn’t before: it wasn’t the city that would destroy us. It was the cracks in this house.

Chapter 3

Whispers of War


Faith


The villa pretended to be calm. That was its newest lie.


Breakfast arrived on time; shoes lined themselves up like soldiers; the bell in the chapel kept the same patient beat. But quiet in that house wasn’t peace—it was pressure. Dante grew sharper at the edges, all steel and schedule. Matteo laughed louder, as if volume alone could hold the walls up. Lorenzo spoke less and meant more.


The staff learned to pass through rooms without making ripples. Eyes drifted to the floor, then to me, then away again. Marcella caught my sleeve in the corridor and pressed a sprig of rosemary into my palm.


“For remembrance,” she said. “Men forget what they promised when they’re planning a war.”


“Karras,” I replied.


She didn’t deny it. “Don’t mistake the lull for safety. The sea looked mild before a storm, too.”


News arrived first, as it always did here—not in headlines, but in men. Dante spread a folder on the study desk, the pages neat as a confession he was ready to read aloud. Lorenzo stood with one hand braced on the map, head bowed enough to pass for prayer.


“Karras shifted routes,” Dante said. “Not direct. A Greek intermediary—Vassilis—Iliopoulos Shipping. They were renting their hulls to a Cyprus shell that paid cash and called itself clean. It wasn’t.”


“Ports?” Lorenzo asked.


“Patras to Split. Then down to us.” Dante tapped a triangle on the map. “Two of ours ran quiet last week. One of theirs ran faster than it should have. I’d call it confidence if it weren’t arrogance.”


Matteo strolled in late, all sun and mischief, and dropped into a chair like the world admired him. “We saw the boy at the bar yesterday,” he said, grinning. “Naples thought you’d gone soft because your woman drank water and you let children keep their teeth.”


“Children got fathers,” Dante said without looking up. “Fathers got ideas. Ideas got skiffs.”


Matteo rolled his eyes. “Then let’s break a father. Subito.” He jabbed a finger at the map. “Here. Hit the Split transfer. Burn the name off the hull and send it back to them like a gift.”


“No,” Lorenzo said.


Matteo slouched deeper. “Why keep teaching them arithmetic if fireworks do the job?”


“Because fireworks taught them nothing but spectacle,” Lorenzo said. “And spectacle invited spectators.”


Dante slid a second sheet forward. “We could cut Vassilis on paper. He’s overdue at two banks. Offer oxygen, take leverage. Intercept the Split transfer on the return—light, quiet. Karras doesn’t get a headline; he gets a bill.”


Matteo’s mouth twitched. “So we were buying a Greek.”


“We were buying time,” Dante said.


They looked to Lorenzo. He considered, then nodded once. “We buy Vassilis. We cut Split on the back end. Matteo, you keep Naples loud without setting a match where I can’t see the wind.”


Matteo smiled like a boy denied a toy and planning to steal it later. “Capito. Loud, no flames.” He stood, crooked me a bow. “Principessa. See? I can behave.”


I didn’t answer. He left whistling a tune saints wouldn’t have liked. Dante closed the folder. “I’ll set the calls,” he said. “And the net for Split.”


“Do it,” Lorenzo said. Their eyes met—agreement, trust, a language I was still learning. Dante left without a sound.


When we were alone, Lorenzo lifted his gaze to me. “Come here.”


I crossed to the map because maps were easier than men. He watched me like I was also terrain he intended to understand.


“Why did you say no to Matteo?” I asked.


“Because when you let a man make war his entertainment,” he said, “he forgets that war pays in names.”


“Paolo,” I said.


His jaw tightened. “Paolo.” The house watched me watch him. In the hallway by the east stairs, two guards I knew by their shoulders argued in their whispering way.


“She makes him soft,” one said. “Did you see him in the city? He let the kid at the bar breathe.”


“She makes him precise,” the other answered. “He hasn’t missed a step since Athens.”


“Soft is a step.”


“Precision is survival.”


They noticed me and shut their mouths like doors. I kept walking because choosing which version of myself to be for them wasn’t an option that day.


In the kitchen, the women fell into a hush that wasn’t hostile so much as evaluative. Not cruelty—calculation. Marcella’s kingdom measured by teacups and grief. She caught my eye and tilted her head toward the garden. I followed, grateful for citrus and air.


“You heard them,” she said among the trees. “Good. If you hadn’t, I’d worry you were in love.”


The word still felt like contraband. “If I was?”


“Then be useful,” she said. “To him and to yourself. Don’t be a mirror. Be a lens.”


“How?” I asked, too quickly.


“See what he sees,” she said. “And then see the thing he misses, because men who hold the map forget to look up at the conditions.”


He took me to the elements that night.


No doors locked. No secrets disguised as kindness. He drew me into the study after the house had swallowed its last whisper and showed me the world he refused to name aloud anywhere else.


“Here,” he said, and the word was gentler than it had any right to be.


The desk was a topography of risk: satellite photos, port schedules, ledger lines written in Dante’s tidy hand. Lines ran like veins from Naples to places with names that tasted of salt and bureaucracy. He stood at my shoulder, not touching, but present enough to change the temperature of my skin.


“This is how war breathed,” he said. “Not with guns only. With time, fuel, men who could be bought and wouldn’t stay bought. With cousins and favors and the price of diesel.”


“You let me see this,” I said, and it wasn’t a question.


“I needed you to,” he said simply. “If you were in the dark, they would make you a story. If you knew the map, you were a piece I couldn’t afford to lose.”


“Useful,” I said, half to myself.


“Loved,” he answered, and it was barely sound at all.


The word landed like a match in wet wood—no sudden blaze, just a steady, stubborn heat. I traced a line with my finger. “Why Split?”


“Because Karras thought we wouldn’t look behind the curtain if the stage was clean,” he said. “We make him pay for arrogance; we don’t give him an audience.”


“And Vassilis?”


“He survived us,” Lorenzo said. “He didn’t own us. He learned the difference.”


I turned to him. “And Matteo?”


“He learned louder,” he said, and something like weariness crossed his face. “Dante could keep him inside the lines—until the lines were too pretty to obey.”


He reached past me for a pen, and the brush of his shoulder along my arm felt more intimate than half the kisses we’d ever shared. It was such a small thing. It undid me.


“Lorenzo,” I said.


He looked up—present, fully, the way only he did, like nothing else in the world deserved his eyes until I did.


“Don’t show me this if you don’t mean it,” I said. “Don’t bind me to your war if you can’t also bind me to your peace.”


He was quiet a heartbeat. Two. Then: “I had no peace without you.”


No recital. No vow. Just a fact that hurt more than comfort ever could.


He kissed me there in the lamplight—slow, consuming, not the storm of last night but something that seeped—like warmth through stone, like water finding its way. His hands held my face as if we were truce and territory both. I answered with the kind of surrender that wasn’t capitulation; it was choice.


He didn’t rush. He unlearned urgency on my mouth. He taught me the way his breath changed when I said his name like a secret I’d decided to keep. When he finally gathered me in—arms around my waist, forehead to mine—I felt more claimed than any time he’d pressed me to a wall. Binding could be gentle. That knowledge frightened me more than the other kind.


“Stay,” he said, so soft no one else could have heard it.


“I wasn’t leaving,” I whispered.


“Good,” he said, and the relief in it was a raw thing I would never mock.


The phone fractured the night. Dante’s voice, trimmed to the bone: “Vassilis bit. He wanted a call at dawn. Also, Matteo moved his meeting to the waterfront. He invited an audience.”


Lorenzo exhaled like a man setting a weight down so he could pick up a heavier one. “You and I take Vassilis. Send two to Split with our offer and their ears open. I’ll deal with Matteo after breakfast.”


“Public or private?” Dante asked.


“Private,” Lorenzo said. “Until I need it public.”


A beat. “Capito.”


The line died. The room remembered us. He looked at me, and for once I didn’t ask to be spared. “Tell me where I stood,” I said.


“Here,” he answered. “Next to me. Close enough to hear what hurts and what helps.”


“I couldn’t stop Matteo.”


“No,” he said. “But you could keep me from breaking him in a way that tore the house.”


“That wasn’t the same as saving him.”


“It was better,” he said. “It saved us.”


I didn’t know if I believed him. I knew I wanted to.


“Show me again,” I said, touching the map, the ledger, the life he’d let me see. “All of it. The lines you wouldn’t cross and the ones you would.”


He did. We stood over paper and power and the stubborn miracle of two people trying to make a treaty inside a war. He told me things he shouldn’t. I learned them like prayer. And when he kissed me later—hands in my hair, mouth sure and slow—I felt the change as clearly as I felt a storm turn: I was no longer the guest in his house or the rumor Naples traded for sport.


I was a piece on the board. Pieces that didn’t move got broken. Pieces that did could still be sacrificed.


I lay awake after he slept, listening to the villa’s careful breathing, to the sea worrying the cliff, to the candle in the chapel gutter and catch. I thought about Marcella’s lens and Dante’s lines and Matteo’s fire and Vassilis’s price and the way Lorenzo’s voice had wrapped around loved like it was a weapon he’d finally decided to use.


Morning would bring calls and meetings and the particular discipline of pretending fear was strategy. But for now, I pressed the rosemary to my lips and let the oil stain my fingers.


Remembrance. For the promise he had made without making it. For the weather I had agreed to watch. For the wire we were all standing on, pretending we weren’t already balancing.


In Naples, quiet was never safe. It was just the breath we took before the whisper became war.

Chapter 4

A Knife in the Dark


Faith


The villa never really slept. It pretended.


After midnight the lights stepped down to a hush, chandeliers dimmed to halos, marble swallowing footsteps like a secret. Men switched radios to a softer channel, the kind you felt in your teeth instead of your ears. Doors remembered how to close without announcing it. Somewhere, the chapel’s candle waged its private war against night.


I wandered because stillness felt like an accusation. If I lay in bed I’d hear all the things the house refused to say out loud. So I took the long hall that smelled faintly of beeswax and orange blossom and counted the breaths between the guards’ passes like beads.


Voices lived in the corners even when mouths were quiet.


“…not all of them are ours,” a guard murmured near the second landing.


“Whose then?” another replied, low.


“The kind that eat where we feed them,” the first said, and the click of a magazine being seated turned the metaphor into a map.


I kept walking. The garden called in the way of places that remembered sun even when the moon was all that was left. Marcella found me before I found it—of course she did. The greenhouse glass threw our reflections back at us, two women the house might have mistaken for saints if it hadn’t known better.


“You shouldn’t roam,” she said, setting a tray of seedlings down as if she were placing a boundary. “Not tonight.”


“What was different about tonight?”


“Nothing,” she said. “And that was the problem.”


I pressed my palm to the cool glass. “You think betrayal circled.”


“I thought it was patient,” she answered. “And that it rarely announced itself twice.”


“Twice?”


She tipped her head toward the house. “Once at the pier. Once at dinner. Do not give it a third.”


She gave me a sprig of mint, not rosemary this time. “For clarity,” she said, and kissed my forehead like she wasn’t supposed to.


I promised her I’d go straight back. I meant it when I said it. I lied to the air ten steps later because the chapel pulled like gravity.


Inside, the candle burned clean, stubborn. Someone had left a photograph tucked into the wood—Paolo at eight, missing front teeth, holding a fish as if he’d caught a car. I touched the frame and told him what I wished someone had told him last week: that some men lived around storms and some men lived inside them and the second kind got struck more.


I turned for the corridor, mind on saints and storms and mint pressed between my fingers, and that was when the villa dropped its pretense.


A shadow broke wrong near the end of the hall.


It moved against the light instead of through it, a piece of darkness that didn’t belong. I stopped because my body knew to. The figure hesitated, as if my stillness were louder than a scream. There was a breath—mine or his, I couldn’t tell—and then the world narrowed to the flash of something slender and hungry in his hand.


Knife.


I should have run. I didn’t. Fear anchored me to the stone, and for a split second I saw it—his path, the angle, the way his shoulder turned to accommodate the first strike. He wasn’t there for me. His body knew another body’s height, another man’s reach. He’d rehearsed this for Lorenzo. I was a complication that had appeared where the choreography said empty air.


“Hey,” I said, for no reason, because sound proved I existed.


He lunged, not at me but past me, the blade seeking a shadow that wasn’t there. My shoulder grazed stone as I twisted; the knife kissed air where my ribs had been a breath earlier. He recalibrated—fast, trained—and this time the blade pivoted to me because I’d seen him, because witnesses were the second thing they told you to remove.


The radio crack at the far end of the hall saved me. Rocco’s voice—two words and half a curse—hit the intruder harder than my scream would have. The man’s head snapped; his body followed the noise. Rocco was already a blur, boots eating marble, hands bigger than the knife knew how to account for.


The impact shook the corridor.


Rocco took the wrist, twisted hard enough to make tendon complain, and drove the man into the wall with the sort of careful cruelty professionals used when they still wanted a throat for talking later. The knife clattered. It was a bright sound that made the rest of the night go dull. The intruder fought like a man who didn’t have a second plan—fast, mean, desperate, using knees and head and whatever god he’d never prayed to. Rocco absorbed, returned, calculated. A hard shoulder. A heel behind an ankle. The kind of pain you could walk away from if someone let you.


“Alive,” Rocco grunted, to himself or to the ghost of Lorenzo’s orders.


Alive lasted three seconds.


The attacker produced a second blade from a sheath at the small of his back—small, black, the color of desperation. It flashed toward Rocco’s ribs. Rocco dropped, stole the man’s balance, and the knife found stone. Sparks framed their struggle like cheap fireworks.


“Down!” another voice snapped—Dante’s—and the corridor obeyed. The attacker didn’t. He pivoted, almost elegant, cutting for an opening that wasn’t there.


The shot was small and mean, not cinematic. Dante’s pistol coughed and the man folded. Rocco caught him with the same hands he’d used to break him and lowered him to the floor in a gesture that looked like respect from a distance.


Silence after violence was thick as felt. My heart beat in my mouth. The mint sprig was crushed between my fingers; its scent went sharp, medicinal.


Dante knelt, checked a pulse he already knew wasn’t there, and looked up at me like I was a piece of evidence the room had forgotten to tag.


“You all right?” he asked.


My nod was a lie my body let me get away with.


Rocco was already scanning the angles, the shadow lines, his breath steadying like a machine returning to idle. “He knew the pattern,” he said. “He knew the passes.”


Dante’s jaw tightened. “Then he didn’t come over the wall.”


He had come through a door men with keys used.


The thought turned the corridor colder.


“Take her,” Dante said to Rocco, and the big man didn’t argue. He put a hand between my shoulder blades—not rough, not gentle—and steered me out of the corridor that had learned a new story about itself.


We didn’t make it ten paces before Lorenzo arrived.


He was all blade and breathing, tie gone, sleeves rolled, the map of his forearms a road I knew too well. He took in the scene with one pass of his eyes: the smear on the stone, the stillness on the floor, the way my hand shook around crushed mint.


“Faith,” he said, and it sounded like a prayer made with teeth.


“I’m fine,” I said, because I knew which words kept rooms from exploding.


He looked at Dante. Dante gave him the only answer that mattered: “Inside.”


Matteo appeared in the doorway to the stairwell, late even to danger, smile sharp as if it had been cut on purpose. “Someone forgot to RSVP,” he said, too loud. “Maybe your guards were flirting.”


“Not tonight,” Dante warned.


“Especially tonight,” Matteo countered, and his gaze slid over me, not sexual, strategic. “You see? This was what happened when pets roamed.”


Lorenzo didn’t move. He didn’t need to. “Say another word,” he told Matteo without heat, “and it will be your last polite one for a month.”


Marcella arrived like a verdict wrapped in silk. She took one look at the floor, at me, at the men, and closed her eyes as if she could sew the night shut with a sigh. When she opened them, she was all function. “Take her hand,” she told Lorenzo. “And for the love of God, don’t shout where the walls can hear you.”


He didn’t shout. He was quieter than I’d ever seen him, which was a worse storm.


In the study he paced once, twice, and then grabbed me like salvation, hands on my face, thumbs at my jaw, mouth brutal with need he didn’t know how to dress up as anything else. I opened because I wanted to, because a part of me that didn’t care about sense needed to prove I was still here too.


He kissed like a man who had arrived too late to a scene and found my body on the floor. He kissed like penance, like rage, like relief that had nails. His breath was hot and wrecked. The sound he made when I said his name was a wound.


“Don’t ever do that again,” he said against my mouth, against my throat, against the pulse he was learning with his teeth. “Don’t walk alone. Don’t stand where corridors narrow. Don’t be anywhere I am not.”


“You can’t be everywhere,” I managed, palms flat to his chest, feeling the hammer of his heart.


“Then be where I am,” he said, and lifted me. The desk greeted me like a witness already sworn in. Papers slid. The lamp shook. He tore the night off me in strips, not clothes—fear, leftover adrenaline, the stink of what could have been.


It wasn’t gentle. It wasn’t careless either. It was the kind of intimacy that burned because love had found a fuse and used it. I wrapped my legs around him and anchored him with my hands, nails biting his shoulders. He cursed into my mouth like prayer, like praise, like a man who had nearly lost the only leverage he gave a damn about and intended to remind fate who she was dealing with.


“Say you’re mine,” he demanded, voice a scrape.


“I’m yours,” I told him, because I was—because pretending otherwise would have been a lie that might get someone killed.


“And you stay with me,” he said, moving, shaping the word stay with his body and his mouth until it became an agreement I felt in my bones.


“Yes.” It was a vow. It tasted like surrender. It tasted like survival.


He broke apart with me like penance paid and owed again tomorrow. After, he didn’t let me go. He held me too tight and not enough, his face in my hair, his breath turning my skin into a home for the second time that night.


“I would have torn the house down,” he said into my shoulder, terrifying because he meant physically. “I would have broken it stone by stone until it told me who put a knife in my corridor.”


“You still might,” I whispered.


“Yes,” he said. “But not tonight.”


We breathed until breathing felt less like thieving.


He stepped back to look at me, to inventory injuries I didn’t have, to tally whatever gods he sometimes pretended not to believe in. “You don’t leave my side,” he said, low, final. “Not until I know which of my doors betrays me.”


“Then show me every door,” I answered. “So I know which ones to close for you.”


Something in his face shifted—surprise, then something more dangerous: trust. He took my hand and pressed it to his mouth like men did with relics and bad ideas they intended to keep. “Stay,” he repeated, softer now, and it sounded like live.


A knock—a single, disciplined tap. Dante. Lorenzo’s mouth hardened; he opened the door without stepping aside.


“Intruder’s dead,” Dante reported. “False patches. No wallet. Hands clean—prints burned off. But the knife is from inside.”


“Inside,” Lorenzo repeated, the word a temperature drop. “From where.”


“Maintenance armory,” Dante said. “Not the main locker. The one for contractors. The logbook was… creative.”


“Fix it,” Lorenzo said. “And tighten the passes. Nobody move alone. Not even me.”


Dante’s glance flicked to me—sober, assessing. “Especially her,” he said. “Matteo was already telling a story.”


“What story,” I asked.


“That you’re the reason we’re soft,” Dante answered without malice. “Or the reason Lorenzo will cut the wrong man first.”


“I’ll give the right one,” Lorenzo said, and the promise was so flat it scared me more than a threat.


Dante nodded once and left the way fire left a room when less honest heat remained.


I didn’t sleep. The house didn’t either. It inhaled and exhaled like a beast learning which limb had been wounded. Somewhere near dawn, a woman sang in the kitchen in a language that turned grief into a lullaby. The candle in the chapel lowered itself with dignity and kept burning.


Lorenzo didn’t let me out of sight. Not to the window. Not to the door. When I stood, his hand found my wrist; when I sat, his shoulder found mine. It wasn’t a leash. It was a human answer to an inhuman equation: keep what you could hold.


The sun found the sea slow, as if the night needed coaxing to end. When it did, the villa looked the way it always did—beautiful, expensive, innocent of its own history. The stain in the corridor was gone; the story remained.


Matteo caught me on the stairs with a smile that didn’t reach anywhere it should. “We’re getting jumpy,” he said. “Next you’ll be wearing body armor to breakfast.”


“Better than wearing your bad decisions,” I said before I could stop myself.


His grin sharpened. “Careful, principessa. Sharp tongues cut the wrong throats.”


“Careful, Matteo,” I returned. “Sharp boys get dull fast.”


Dante’s voice from below: “Enough before I make it official.”


Matteo laughed and went to pour coffee strong enough to strip paint. He’d keep needling. Dante would keep damping. Lorenzo would keep drawing lines on maps and faces.


I went back to the study because that was where he was. He looked up as if he never looked away. The line between his brows was a story I wanted to rewrite and couldn’t.


“Stay,” he said, softer than last night.


“I wasn’t leaving,” I told him.


I meant it. I didn’t want out. I wanted him. I wanted the man who would burn a city down for me and I wanted the man who had learned how not to. I wanted the cage that was also a shield and the chain that was also a vow. Want was a dangerous word there. It was also the truest.


I looked at his hands—bloodless now, steady. I heard the echo of steel on stone, of Dante’s shot, of my own pulse screaming at a shadow that had known his height. I understood something I hadn’t let myself say before:


I was not just marked by him anymore.


I was targeted because of him.


The cage was shrinking. I leaned into it.


If it killed me, I thought, it would do so with my eyes open and his name in my mouth—and in that house, that might have been the only kind of mercy we got.

Chapter 5

The Cost of Loyalty


Faith


The villa was a coffin dressed like a palace.


Doors clicked too firmly, guards doubled at every corridor, radios spat code instead of words. Even laughter—the rare, brittle kind that leaked from Matteo’s mouth—sounded like treason. Since the knife found its way inside these walls, nothing here breathed without suspicion.


I felt it most in the way the staff moved. Their shoulders curved inward, their eyes slid sideways, their hands obeyed with exaggerated quiet. Even Marcella’s kitchen, usually the only room in this house that smelled of peace—lemons, rosemary, butter—had dulled. A young maid dropped a pan and flinched like she’d fired a gun.


Marcella pressed a sprig into my hand before I sat at the breakfast table. Thyme, green and sharp.


“For courage without noise,” she said, her voice low.


I wanted to ask if courage was enough. She answered before I spoke. “Don’t look away when he makes you want to. That’s your weapon.”


I didn’t understand until later.


Breakfast was not breakfast. It was drama.


The family sat as if this table were the altar it has always pretended to be. Lorenzo at the head, silent and sharpened. Dante at his right hand, face clean, mind working like an accountant of death. Matteo sprawled on his left, smile mocking, the crackle of defiance too close to laughter. Marcella sat, her rosary wound tight enough to cut her fingers.


The staff and guards lined the walls like saints painted on frescos—stiff, flat, pretending piety, praying not to be noticed.


“Someone opened a door,” Lorenzo said. He did not raise his voice. He never has to. “Someone fed my house to wolves. I’ll know who.”


The silence was the kind that tastes of iron.


Dante began with the ledger, flipping pages with precision. “Door rotations. Shift covers. Maintenance reports. Logbooks.” His voice was cool, clinical. “Too many coincidences.”


He handed Lorenzo names like knives. Lorenzo cut with questions—short, exacting. Who was on duty at corridor six? Who signed for maintenance at the east armory? Why was a key logged back late?


Each man answered with trembling formality. Their eyes never met his.


Until Nico.


He was the youngest guard. Barely more than a boy, with the kind of jaw that hadn’t decided whether to be soft or hard. He rubbed his fingers together constantly, like he was worried the world might notice the weight of coins.


When Lorenzo asked him who swapped duty on the chapel wing, his eyes darted left before his mouth shaped a right.


“Again,” Lorenzo said, voice like frost. “Who was on chapel duty?”


“Beni,” Nico stammered.


Dante didn’t look up. “Beni buried his father that night. I have the death certificate. I have the florist receipt. You signed the condolence card.”


The boy’s mouth worked. His hands shook harder. Matteo grinned, slow and cruel.


“Try again,” Lorenzo said.


Nico broke. The lie splintered, and his voice poured out in a rush. “My mother’s rent! I didn’t open the door. I just looked away. Just once.” His eyes darted like a cornered animal. “A man came, said if I gave him one night—one minute—he’d take care of us. I swear I didn’t open the door. I just… I just didn’t stop it.”


The air grew heavy enough to crush bone.


“How much,” Dante asked, clinical.


“Two thousand euros,” Nico whispered. “Cash. It’s still under my mattress. I swear—I didn’t spend it. I didn’t—”


“Enough,” Lorenzo cut in. His eyes were knives. He stripped the pistol from Nico’s belt and laid it on the table as calmly as a man setting down cutlery. “Your weakness cost me trust. "Hanno messo in pericolo mia moglie. Questo… non lo perdonerò."


(They put my wife in danger. This… I will not forgive.).” His hand flicked toward me. “That debt doesn’t get repaid.”


“Please—” Nico fell to his knees, begging. “Don, per favore. My mother—she doesn’t know. She’ll die without me—”


Lorenzo’s voice was colder than stone. “Better she bury a son than a coward.”


The words landed like a guillotine.


He nodded to Rocco. The big man stepped forward, hauled Nico to his feet like he weighed no more than the shame on his shoulders. The boy cried, pleaded, swore on saints he’d probably never prayed to.


They dragged him into the courtyard.


We followed. We had to. That was the point.


The courtyard smelled of wet stone, of old roses, of endings. Nico fell to his knees again, sobbing, face streaked with salt.


“Please!” he begged, eyes darting everywhere—at me, at Marcella, at anyone who might have had pity left to spend. “I didn’t mean—”


Lorenzo’s jaw never moved. “Meaning doesn’t matter. Doors opened.”


He gave Rocco the gun.


The shot cracked through the courtyard. Short. Final. The pigeons scattered from the roof in a rush of wings. Nico crumpled against the stone like a toy dropped from too high.


Silence afterward was absolute.


Lorenzo looked at the rest of us—at every guard, at every staff member, at me—and said only: “This is the cost of looking away.”


Then he walked inside, as if execution were nothing more than another appointment in his day.


That night, I felt the echo of that bullet in my bones.


I sat on the edge of the bed, thyme still crushed in my palm, and wondered if courage meant refusing to look away—or surviving when you couldn’t.


The door opened with force.


Lorenzo entered, stripped down to shirt sleeves, hair undone, face carved by a fury he’d been holding all day. He didn’t speak. He crossed the room in three strides and took my face in his hands like he was starving for oxygen.


His mouth crashed into mine, brutal and consuming. Not cruelty—terror turned inside out. He kissed me like a man who had almost found me dead and needed to bury the ghost before it buried him.


“You don’t walk alone,” he rasped against my lips, his breath hot and ragged. “You don’t breathe without me. You belong to me. Do you understand?”


“Yes.” My voice trembled, but my body arched into him with defiance, with want, with surrender.


He tore at my dress like silk had dared insult him, the fabric ripping like a shriek. My back hit the wall with a jarring thud, his hands everywhere, branding. His mouth scorched my throat, then dragged across my collarbone, teeth scraping skin. I tasted the salt of his desperation. His control cracked, every line of him shaking with fear transmuted into a feral, demanding desire.


“You’re mine,” he growled as he lifted me, pressing me into the plaster. His knee shoved between my legs, forcing my stance wide, and I whimpered—half defiance, half pure, begging relief. “Say it, Faith. Say you belong only here.”


“I’m yours,” I gasped, nails scoring his back. His hard heat was a non-negotiable weight against my center. “Always.”


He took me hard, desperate. One hand slammed flat above my head, anchoring me to the wall, while the other seized my hip, tilting me up for his depth. Every thrust was a vow, a claim written in burning friction, every kiss a curse against fate. He drove in, deep and unrelenting, stealing the breath from my lungs. The sound was wet, primal, and sickeningly loud in the quiet room. I wrapped around him, clinging, moaning his name until the sound became prayer, my slick core gripping him, begging him to shatter me. He made love like war—violent, raw, and terrifyingly tender beneath the ruin.


When he came undone, it was with my wrists trapped above my head and a vow in his teeth: “No one takes you from me and lives.”


After, he didn’t let go. He collapsed with me still pinned against the wall, arms around me like chains, chest heaving. His lips pressed into my hair, softer now, breaking.


“I would have burned this house to the ground if it had touched you,” he whispered hoarsely.


“I know,” I said, trembling, holding him as if that might keep the fire from catching.


And in that moment, I realized what Marcella had meant: he was making me into a symbol. Not weakness. Not softness.


But proof of what happens when the Don loves.


And symbols don’t get to walk free.

Chapter 6

Blood in the Ledger


Faith


The sea at Naples never slept, but it did conspire.


By the time Dante called the meeting, dawn only licked the horizon. The villa held damp in its bones, storm-breathing. Men moved like hounds on a scent none of them could yet name. I followed because Lorenzo told me to, and no one questioned it anymore—not even me.


Dante opened a folder with the neat fatalism of a priest laying out last rites. “Shipment from Patras,” he said. “Twelve containers. Nine arrived. Three are missing. Paperwork says otherwise. Paperwork lies.”


Lorenzo hadn’t slept; I could tell by the way his knuckles planted on the desk, veins rising like topography. “Karras?”


“Smells like him.” Dante’s tone was dry enough to leave a rasp. “He laundered the theft through Split. Paid small men to forget small oaths. The dockmaster’s nephew discovered higher education this week.”


Matteo lounged like a sin in a pew, flipping a coin that flashed light like a blade. “Why pay nephews when fingers come cheaper? One joint for every missing crate. Men remember their hands.”


“We remember headlines,” Dante said without looking at him. “Headlines invite Rome.”


“We don’t blink,” Lorenzo said, the decision a temperature.


Matteo smiled. “So we burn.”


“We strategized,” Dante returned, that single s making the word a weapon.


They stared at each other—two answers to the same question, neither forgiving the other for existing. Lorenzo let the silence harden until even the walls learned posture. Then he looked at me.


“You’ll come,” he said. Not a question.


“Where?”


“The docks,” he said. “If you’re mine, you see what’s mine. All of it.”


Marcella stood at the threshold, rosary quiet as rain. She crossed herself. “Don’t take her to salt and steel.”


“She belonged in both,” Lorenzo answered, already standing.


⸻


The docks smelled of diesel, brine, and men who had made peace with splinters. Steel containers stacked like a crude cathedral, graffiti scabbed on their sides—prayers in paint, curses in symbols. Soldiers and stevedores slipped through the aisles, each man wearing a purpose the way other men wore jackets.


When eyes found me, the air shifted. Some looked away fast, as though I were holy. Others lingered, measuring how long until the Don’s woman became the Don’s mistake. I felt every look like a thumb pressed into a bruise, so I let my chin learn marble.


“Don’t be a mirror,” Marcella had said. “Be glass that cuts.”


Dante walked us through the numbered rows, reciting routes and manifests in a voice built for balance sheets and funerals. Red chalk slashed three empty places along a loading map; the chalk dust looked like dried mercy.


“This artery feeds the city,” he said. “Money, steel, silence. When something disappears, it isn’t loss; it’s insult.”


Matteo kicked a crate and let the echo travel. “Then answer in kind. Loud. Memorable.”


“Loud is sloppy,” Dante said. “Sloppy invites Rome.”


“And whispers invite wolves,” Matteo snapped. “Karras plays games because you write letters with your eyes.”


He moved faster than the talk: one fist in Dante’s lapel, a smile too bright. Dante didn’t flinch. For a heartbeat they looked like enemies who had spent a lifetime practicing how to be brothers.


“Enough.” Lorenzo didn’t raise his voice. The word cracked harder than a gunshot.


Matteo let go, hands up, grin intact, eyes saying Not finished. Dante smoothed the cloth where fingers had wrinkled it, and all the men around us watched the film of this family play on their private screens.


Lorenzo turned, and the docks shifted to accommodate his gravity. “We lift every seal. We pull camera feeds from Split to Naples. We buy the dockmaster’s priest and nephew both. I want vents opened, manifests scrubbed, splinters counted. If those three boxes breathed air anywhere, I want to know what it tasted like.”


He looked at me last. “Stay with Dante.”


It sounded like a kindness. It was a line on a map.


We moved. We climbed catwalks and ducked rusted ladders. Dante flagged the places cameras had blinked too long, and I wrote times on the palm of my hand with a pen that had bled on other men’s names. Twice he asked if I needed to sit. Twice I didn’t.


We found the first clue in a place men forgot to look: not a mark on a container, not a smudge on a ledger—an odor. A trace of sweet chemical beneath the diesel, a fragrance that didn’t belong to salt. Dante closed his eyes, inhaled once, opened them again.


“Solvent,” he said. “Paint thinner. Someone disguised a mark and gave it back its old face.”


By the time we reached the pier-head, the wind had turned mean. Rocco spoke into his radio and the docks listened. A runner brought a phone. Dante took it, said three things that sounded like arithmetic, and handed it back.


“Matteo’s boys found a van,” he said to Lorenzo, as if reporting weather to a man who controlled the sky. “East side—old cannery. Plates false. Tire treads match those two empty minutes by camera four.”


“Matteo is not to move without you,” Lorenzo said.


“He already moved,” Dante answered, mouth a straight line across a bad map.


The Don’s face did something few men had lived to see: it flickered. He turned to Rocco. “Take eight. Go. Bring them both back unbloodied.”


Rocco nodded. The docks inhaled. Vehicles growled. Orders became motion.


“Why show me this?” I asked, when the noise thinned.


“Because they’ll use you if you don’t understand it,” he said. “Because a woman who sees the cost is a woman who knows which bill to pay.” He was looking at the water, but the line found me exactly where it was meant to.


“Then let me pay something,” I said.


He lifted an eyebrow. Dante stared at me, measuring whether I was a problem that solved other problems. Lorenzo tipped his head once. “Pay attention,” he said.


⸻


Back at the villa, the storm he’d been storing finally broke. He shut the study door with his hand around my wrist, not rough, not gentle—true. The desk still wore Dante’s tidy clutter like jewelry on bone.


“You saw how they looked at you,” he said.


“Yes.”


“You saw how my brothers looked at each other.”


“Yes.”


He stepped in, and the space where our breaths met heated the room. The kiss didn’t ask; it arrived—salt, coffee, iron. He tasted like the docks: work and danger and the kind of hunger that didn’t apologize for existing. His mouth demanded an answer; mine gave it. His hands found my waist; my hands found his shoulders; the study brightened into a place where prayer had a body.


“You are not soft,” he told me, his voice hoarse against my mouth. “They will try to make you into a softness to cut me with.”


“Then cut them first,” I said.


“Prove it,” he said, and lifted me onto the desk, scattering ledgers like a forecast finally wrong.


I did.


There was nothing civil about the way we reached for each other. The storm he’d held in front of every man on the docks poured through us now—hot, relentless, not cruelty but the cure for it. I gave as much as I took. He asked for my eyes; I gave them. He told me not to look away; I didn’t. Heat rose without shame—in my throat, in my belly, in the ache that answered his hands. When the world narrowed to rhythm and the sounds we made, he pressed his forehead to mine like a man saying a confession to the only god who could absolve him.


“Mine,” he said, not as a threat. As a solution.


“Yours,” I answered, because I was and because it made the arithmetic come out right.


He didn’t move away when it ended; he held me through the aftershocks, body locked to mine like a door set with a bar. When he finally lifted his head, his voice had sand in it. “I can lose ships. I can lose steel. I can lose men. If I lose you—” He stopped like the words were a cliff.


“That ledger won’t balance,” I finished for him.


He nodded once, a dangerous man agreeing to something softer than mercy. He kissed me again, slower, and gathered me into his lap on the chair, my knees folding to either side of a life no longer pretending to be separate from his.


The knock that came then wasn’t a knock. It was Dante’s voice at the door: “Open,” he said, and Lorenzo did.


“Matteo?” Lorenzo asked.


“Loud,” Dante replied. “Alive. The van was empty. But the cannery gave us a scent.” He set a brown envelope on the desk. Photographs slid out—blurred plates, a smear of paint thinner on a crate, the edge of a glove too clean for dock work.


“And?” Lorenzo’s tone sharpened.


“A name,” Dante said. “Not Karras. A cousin to the cousin—Vassilis’s foreman. Paid twice. Once by him. Once by men we don’t yet know.”


Matteo appeared behind Dante, hair wild, smile wilder. He leaned against the doorjamb without asking permission. “The men we don’t yet know carry Greek cigarettes,” he said. “And wear shoes they can’t afford.”


“Where are they now?” Lorenzo asked.


“Running,” Matteo said, delighted. “I gave them a reason.”


Lorenzo stared at him until delight remembered consequences. “You were told not to move.”


“I was told a lot of things,” Matteo said, light as gasoline vapor. “But I brought back something useful.” He flicked another photograph onto the desk. It showed a strip of tape peeled from a crate—beneath it, numbers had been reprinted in a different font.


Dante looked at the picture like it had confessed to a crime he already knew about. “They copied labels. The solvent took the edge off the ink. If we pull the right angles from the cameras in Split, we’ll see the hands that did it.”


“Then pull them,” Lorenzo said.


“I already am,” Dante answered, and slid his eyes to me. Something in his gaze had changed. Not trust. Not yet. But the outline of it.


“Anything else?” Lorenzo asked.


“Two things,” Dante said. “The nephew’s tuition came from an account in Cyprus. And the foreman’s girlfriend bought a ring last week.”


“Men who intend to live long buy tools,” Lorenzo said. “Men who intend to run buy rings.”


Matteo grinned. “Men who intend to die buy tickets.”


“Enough,” Lorenzo said again, but the word landed differently this time—less fury, more focus. He pointed once, carving the air. “Dante, you handle Split. Buy the camera servers if you must. Buy the man who plugs them in. Rocco, lock down the maintenance armory and the contractor keys. Nobody moves alone. Not me, not her.”


His eyes cut to me, a slash of ownership and apology both. I nodded once. Agreement wasn’t surrender; it was strategy.


“Matteo,” Lorenzo said last. “You keep Naples loud—without starting a fire I didn’t ask for.”


Matteo’s smile tilted. “What if the fire asks for you?”


“Then I put my hand in it,” Lorenzo said, “and teach it manners.”


The day tilted toward copper. Naples turned its face to the wind. The villa held its breath and counted footsteps like prayer beads. When the call came at sundown, Dante didn’t bother with prelude.


“Split’s footage is ours,” he said over the speakerphone. “We have the men. One of them wears a patch he didn’t earn.” A soft rustle, a photograph turning. “Karras is borrowing other crews.”


“Who?” Lorenzo asked.


A beat. “People who think we won’t look behind the curtain if the stage is clean.” Dante’s voice had satisfaction in it now, thin as a knife edge. “Iliopoulos’s foreman met with a broker out of Dubrovnik. Not Greek. Not Italian.”


“Then he’s nobody,” Matteo said. “And nobodies bleed like everybody else.”


“Name?” Lorenzo asked, ignoring his brother’s pleasure.


“Petrovic,” Dante said. “A courier with friends who like to be paid twice.”


“Good,” Lorenzo said. “Pay him once. Then charge him for the second.”


I watched the machine move then: the way a word became a plan became a phone call became a car engine. The way men in suits learned how to be weather. The way a Don did math with lives and kept the column of mercy shockingly short.


When the study emptied and the house settled into the kind of quiet that hardly ever meant peace, I stood at the map beside him and traced a line I had no business touching.


“Here,” I said, finger against Split. “If they stole there and we only felt it here, the hand is longer than we think.”


He looked at my hand instead of the map. “What do you see?”


“That Karras wants you emotional,” I said. “He’s stealing small to make you spend big. Not money. Poise. If you bleed in public, Naples will lap it.”


Something fierce and fond crossed his face, as if the words undid him in a place only I knew how to find. “Stay,” he said, soft enough that a man could call it weakness if he didn’t understand what it cost him to ask.


“I wasn’t leaving,” I said, and stepped into him.


The second storm of the day didn’t announce itself; it happened. This time, there was nothing frantic in it—only absolute, controlled possession. He waited at the foot of the bed, his dark eyes fixed on me, the silence a heavy, shared weight of expectation.


He crossed the room slowly. He undressed me like he was disarming a bomb, each movement deliberate, a signature of ownership. His touch was sure and patient, his mouth reverent but demanding at the hinge of my throat. I leaned into the heat, already trembling, accepting the quiet, inevitable verdict in his gaze.


"You are my house, my ruin, my only peace," he murmured, his breath hot against my ear as his hands traced the curve of my waist, the pad of his thumb pressing into my hipbone. "This is where I belong, and you will remind me."


I learned him again with my hands, every line of his back, every place his breath caught when I said his name. I raked my nails lightly over the hard muscles of his shoulders, pulling his body to mine until no air remained between us.


When he finally took me, burying himself in a single, unhurried, agonizing stroke, it wasn’t violence; it was verdict. It was the Don claiming his only territory, his possession wrapped in worship. The deep, grinding rhythm turned my body into a promise I wanted to keep—a vow of surrender. He pinned my hips to the sheets, dominating the movement, demanding everything I was.


When the pressure became unbearable, I cried out a sound that belonged only to him, and I shattered, the climax ripping through me in waves of molten heat. "Look at me," he commanded, his eyes burning into mine, forcing me to meet the intensity of his claim. I did, and stayed there while he followed, his own release a ragged, visceral groan, his breath breaking against my mouth like tides against stone.


After, he folded me into him and watched the window with the eyes he saved for war. “You wanted to pay something,” he murmured into my hair. “You already did.”


“What?” I asked.


“You stayed,” he said. “You looked. You didn’t flinch.”


I rested my cheek over his heartbeat. “Then send me a bill,” I said.


“I will,” he said, and there was a smile in it I almost never got to keep.


Near midnight, the chapel’s candle burned shorter, stubborn as a saint. His phone buzzed on the nightstand with a message Dante didn’t send unless necessity wrote it.


Split confirms Petrovic. Meeting scheduled. Two days. Waterfront. Their terms. Our terms: we pick the ground. I showed it to Lorenzo. He read it and kept reading what wasn’t there.


“Two days,” he said, thinking in miles and pressure. “Good. It gives us time to prepare a stage.”


“For a theater or a courtroom?” I asked, remembering Rocco’s old joke.


He brushed his thumb down my jaw, the smallest touch turning into a binding. “A church,” he said. “Where vows are made. And broken.”


He slept then, the kind of sleep a man takes by force. I lay awake and listened while the villa pretended to rest and the sea gnawed its patient way into stone. I had stopped being just the Don’s woman. I had become an entry in the ledger—a power and a price at once.


And in this house, ledger entries always ended in blood.

Chapter 7

The Debt Collector


Lorenzo


Naples woke like a city that knew it owed me an answer.


By the time the chapel’s candle burned down to a stub, Dante had strung lines through half the coast. Phones that never rang began to purr. Men who never prayed crossed themselves when they passed our gates. The villa breathed shallow, counting its heartbeats.


Petrović.


A courier with ambition and a borrowed army. A man who sold routes the way other men sold fruit—loud, quick, and rotten underneath. Karras liked to stay pretty while someone else did the work. Petrović was someone else.


“Two days,” Dante said, eyes on the whiteboard he’d built across the study wall. He wrote like a surgeon—no wasted strokes. “We choose ground. We control angles. We keep the story quiet.”


Matteo tossed a knife into the tabletop and made a game of letting it stick on the edge. “Or we let the story sing.”


Dante didn’t look up. “Songs invite Rome.”


“Rome already listens,” Matteo said. “Give them something worth hearing.”


I let them tire themselves against each other for a minute longer, then drew the room to orbit me. “We take the waterfront, warehouse thirty-seven.” I pointed at a photographed rectangle of concrete and rust. “One road in, two doors out. The third is welded. Rocco, I want a perimeter in plain clothes, eyes inside the trucks. No masks. No armor that prints under shirts. This is a meeting until it isn’t.”


Rocco grunted assent.


“Dante,” I continued, “you’ll manage the camera blackout. Not all of them—only the ones that matter. Leave a few pointed at harmless corners. I want the footage that survives to tell the story we like.”


“And the story we like is…?” Dante asked.


“That Petrović came to apologize,” I said. “And he left lighter than he arrived.”


Matteo smiled, slow, greedy. “Lighter how?”


“Of time,” I said. “Of options.” I let my gaze rest on my brother until the coin in his palm forgot how to flip. “Not of decency. We don’t make spectacle.”


“Spectacle keeps a city still,” he said.


“Spectacle keeps a city hungry,” I countered. “Hungry cities bite.”


We broke the meeting into motion. Men peeled off, taking my orders with them. The house resumed its measured breathing. The sea chewed the cliff and pretended patience.


Faith stood in the doorway with her hair pinned up and a notebook in her hand she didn’t hide. She watched the board like she’d always spoken this language and only just remembered it.


“Come,” I told her, and when she crossed to the table the others moved without needing to be told. I put a fingertip on Split and drew a line to Naples. “Explain it back to me.”


She didn’t flinch. “You want him emotional,” she said. “You let him think you’ve accepted his terms, then make him accept yours. Not once. Twice. First by ground. Then by story.”


Dante’s eyebrow ticked, the smallest nod. He was learning the shape of the use she could be.


Matteo made a sound that wasn’t quite a laugh. “Or we could shoot him in the head.”


Faith didn’t look at him. “And make Karras untouchable by letting him hide behind grief? No. Make him jealous.” She met my eyes. “Make him watch you be patient.”


There it was again—that clear, quiet blade. Not softness. Glass that cut.


I put my hand over hers on the map because I could. Because the room needed to see that I could. “Stay close,” I said. “You’ll see how patience pays.”


We trained without calling it training. Rocco took six men to the old car barn and drilled entry patterns until their shoulders forgot how to argue. Dante spent an hour in the chapel with two laptops and a grim little smile, kneeling like a penitent with wires for rosary beads. Matteo vanished and returned with exactly the kind of ammunition I had told him not to touch and exactly the grin that said he knew I would use it anyway if I had to.


In the courtyard, I let the men see me break a pistol down and build it again without looking. I let them see me spar with Rocco and take the bruise he gave me like gratitude. I let them see me speak to the cooks and drink coffee with the gardeners and lift Marcella’s crates of lemons like a son who remembered their weight. People are animals; they follow scent. I gave them mine: oil, citrus, iron, and the clean breath of a house that would survive the night.


Faith watched all of it with those eyes that made a man consider being better than the room deserved. When she stepped close, the air around us said private even if the flagstones were crowded.


“You don’t have to do every job,” she murmured. “They have hands.”


“They have hands,” I agreed. “I have the reason.”


She reached for my sleeve and turned it to look at the shadowed bruise there, faint purple under skin. Her thumb made a circle that felt like consent and warning at once. “Don’t spend yourself to prove you can pay.”


That got a laugh out of me I hadn’t expected. “If I stop spending, they’ll stop counting.”


“Then make them count something else.”


“What?”


“Your quiet,” she said. “It’s louder than all this.”


Later, when we had a minute the house didn’t think we owned, I took her to the winter garden where the lemon trees kept their small suns behind glass. She stood between two pots and I stepped in, and the world did that trick it had learned—shrinking around her, bending the night so everything curved toward a single point.


“You asked to pay,” I reminded her.


She lifted her chin. “I still am.”


My thumb found her mouth; her lips parted around the pressure as if the gesture were a language we both remembered. I kissed her there with the restraint of a man who had already decided how he would lose it. She answered with the certainty of a woman who knew what I needed to borrow to make it to morning.


“Look at me,” I asked.


“I can’t look anywhere else,” she said, and the quiet heat of it went through my bones like a clean burn.


When I carried her hand to my chest, she didn’t ask for mercy. She asked for truth. I gave it to her against the inside of her wrist where the pulse lived. “Two days,” I said, and there was promise in it. “Stay with me.”


“Try and stop me.”


I didn’t.


The meeting request came with more arrogance than craft: a courier in a leather jacket who thought posture counted for power. He delivered a card that didn’t have a name, only a time and a place. It was the place I’d already chosen.


“Good,” I told him without looking at the card twice. “Tell your boss I accept his apology.”


The courier swallowed. “He—he didn’t say—”


“He will,” I said, and handed the card back.


Matteo waited until the man had been walked out before he laughed. “You’ll make him kiss your ring next.”


“If I ask for rings,” I said, “it’s to weigh a body down.”


Dante didn’t laugh. “We put eyes inside thirty-seven an hour before he arrives,” he said. “We park three vans where they can’t be seen and leave room for four that can. We ghost the far door. If he runs, he runs where we want.”


“What about the cousin’s cousin,” Matteo asked, all innocence. “Vassilis’s foreman?”


“We don’t touch him yet,” I said. “He thinks he has choices. Men who think they have choices teach you more than men who don’t.”


Faith spoke then, standing by the window where afternoon shook itself into gold. “What do you want Petrović to leave with?”


The room looked at me, but I looked at her. “Fear,” I said. “And the correct impression that it belongs to me now.”


She nodded. “Then don’t raise your voice.”


I didn’t intend to.


Waterfront. Warehouse thirty-seven. Evening so late that it preferred being called night.


We arrived first. We always did. The interior smelled like dust and old starch, the ghosts of sacks long gone and the iron memory of cranes that didn’t work anymore. Our lights were modest. Our men, more so.


Faith walked in step at my left the way a heartbeat walked beside a man and made him look alive. I kept her in my periphery and made sure every face in the room knew that I did. Rocco positioned two at the welded door and two more behind the pallet stacks. Dante disappeared into a shadow that happened to have a camera feed inside it.


I stood where the open concrete made me vulnerable because vulnerability is a performance I could afford for a minute.


Petrović arrived late, of course—men who want to be important do. He came with five and a swagger, a leather jacket zipped too high and a beard he didn’t deserve. The first thing he did was look at Faith. The second thing he did was regret it, because I let him see the exact price of the look.


“Don Bellucci,” he said in a Slavic purr. “Beautiful night.”


“You made it better by keeping your hands visible,” I said. “Apology accepted. Now prove you meant it.”


He blinked like a man who had just been introduced to a cliff. “Apology?”


“You stole boxes that didn’t belong to you,” I said lightly. “That was an error in arithmetic. This is your chance to balance the line.”


He tried a smile built for cheaper rooms. “Business is business.”


“Exactly,” I said. “And business is math. Here is mine.” I raised a hand and Dante’s screen, somewhere I hadn’t told Petrović existed, threw a photo onto the thin white wall behind us: the fake label half-dissolved by solvent; the blurred plate of a van outside the cannery; the foreman’s girlfriend’s new ring.


I didn’t look at the pictures. I watched his eyes read them.


“Not mine,” he said, but his mouth had dried.


“Of course,” I said. “You didn’t steal anything. You simply hosted theft. You rented a corridor and forgot to ask who walked through it. And now you’ve come to say you’re sorry.”


He tried laughter. It sounded like an old hinge. “What do you want?”


I stepped forward one meter. Not two. One. “I want you to ask your hands who pays them twice,” I said. “I want you to return the three boxes I misplaced. I want ten percent of anything that uses your corridor until I find the man Karras keeps behind you. And I want your word that you will never look at my wife again.”


He heard the last one like a man hears a glass fall—too late to catch, just in time to flinch. He flicked a glance at Faith because he didn’t know how not to. It cost him fifty thousand euros and part of an evening.


He tried a different angle. “You ask too much.”


I almost smiled. “You arrived to a meeting in another man’s house, in another man’s city, with another man’s boss in your ear. I am asking very little.”


His man to the left moved an inch. Rocco shifted in a way that made the inch regret itself. Dante’s silence braided around the rafters. Matteo’s grin was a bright knife somewhere I kept just outside the light.


“Ten percent,” Petrović said. “And the boxes.”


“And the apology,” I said. “For my house.”


He swallowed. “For your—house.”


“And for my wife.”


He looked at Faith again, couldn’t help it, and this time the look was clean: no calculation, only fear. “I apologize, signora,” he said, the word thick. “It was… impolite.”


“It was,” Faith said, calm as a saint who knows the bones in the reliquary. “Thank you.”


Dante’s phone vibrated once—the tiniest bell. He didn’t move, but I knew which answer the church had given him. He spoke without speaking: Yes. The boxes were found. The camera feeds were ours. The broker in Dubrovnik had a name that would matter tomorrow.


“Good,” I said to Petrović. “You’re lighter already.”


He exhaled like a man who had expected to lose fingers and discovered he still had a full set. “We’re finished then.”


“Almost,” I said.


His chin tilted. “Almost?”


I stepped close enough to measure his breath. “You will carry a message,” I said. “To the man who pays you to pretend you pay yourself. Tell him this corridor belongs to me now and I charge for traffic. Tell him that if a box opens without my permission between Patras and Naples, I will open something he cares about in return.”


“What?” he asked, throat working.


“His calendar,” I said. “Men die when they don’t show up on time. I will make him late.”


For one dizzy second he didn’t understand. Then he did, and a thin cold lit his eyes that I recognized as respect. “I’ll tell him.”


“You’ll tell him truthfully,” I said, and moved my hand from my side enough that the leather under my jacket whispered yes. “And you will leave now.”


He left. He left quickly. He left with all his fingers and an apology he would have to repeat in a different language.


When the doors shut and the warehouse took us back, Matteo let loose a breath and a compliment at the same time. “Elegant,” he said.


“Effective,” Dante corrected.


Faith turned her face toward the open bay and let the night move across her cheek like the sea giving blessing. She didn’t smile. I felt the current shift inside me—the part of a man that knows when a thing has changed and won’t change back.


“Take her home,” I told Rocco, and the big man didn’t argue. He placed himself at her shoulder like a wall learned how to walk.


Matteo lingered, hunger in his eyes that wasn’t about Faith for once. It was about the heat of a stage. “You could have made him bleed,” he said.


“I did,” I answered. “Just not where you could see it.”


He toyed with the knife again, eyes on the door where Petrović had gone. “Karras will send something prettier next time.”


“Good,” I said. “I’m tired of ugly men.”


The villa didn’t greet me; it absorbed me. Rooms returned to their shapes. The candle in the chapel shortened itself by another hour and refused to die.


I found Faith on the balcony off our room, Naples thrown at her feet like a necklace of dirty lights. She turned when I stepped out, and the grief I keep locked behind my teeth loosened enough to make me human for a moment.


“I didn’t raise my voice,” I told her.


“I know,” she said. “I heard you anyway.”


I stepped in behind her and set my hands at her hips; the city lay before us and listened because it didn’t have a choice. “Petrović carries a message. Karras will read it. And then he will decide whether to be entertained or afraid.”


“What do you want him to be?” she asked.


“Exact,” I said, and bent to her throat because I could, because the night demanded a tithe. She arched into me with the quiet hunger that made my bones remember they were built for more than standing. The heat between us rose the way fires rise in trusted hearths—sure, steady, dangerous in the right wind.


“Stay with me,” I asked against her skin.


“I wasn’t leaving,” she answered, and that was worship, that was vow.


Later, when breath returned and the room remembered it had walls, I lay with her head on my chest and counted the beats that still belonged to me. Outside, Naples whispered to itself about the man who had arrived with five and left with less. Inside, I let my hand rest over her ribs and knew what every ledger in this city refused to balance:


Some debts you paid by living long enough to keep paying.


Dante’s text arrived near dawn.


— Boxes recovered. Dubrovnik broker identified.


— One more name surfaced: Iliopoulos himself wants a meeting. He smells smoke.


I smiled into the ceiling. Not because it was funny. Because it meant the tide had noticed the shore.


“Two days,” I said again, and Faith stirred—only half awake, wholly mine. “We’re going to Split.”


She didn’t open her eyes. “Then bring me my shoes,” she murmured, and fell back into the kind of sleep that trusted me to do the rest.


I would. And I would teach the sea to conspire on our behalf.

Chapter 8

The Fire in Split


Faith


Split took the sea and sharpened it.


The harbor glittered like broken glass under a bruised sky, ferries shouldering the water while gulls screamed judgement from the air. We arrived at dusk, the hour where everything looks honest because it’s too tired to lie. Our convoy nosed through limestone streets that smelled of salt and diesel and cigarettes smoked down to their last bad idea.


Lorenzo looked carved out of night. Dante was the blade laid along its edge. Matteo lounged opposite me in the back seat, a grin in search of gasoline.


“Welcome to the honeymoon,” he said, flicking his coin. “Nothing says romance like a foreign port and borrowed knives.”


I met his gaze until the coin forgot how to turn. “Careful you don’t cut yourself playing with other men’s weapons.”


He laughed. He always did when blood was a possibility.


The restaurant sat on the water’s lip, all glass and stone and the kind of money that doesn’t bother to hide. Inside, music murmured under voices that tried to sound casual. The dining room was a stage: long windows, longer shadows, linen as white as threat. We were early by design. Power looked better when it pretended to be patient.


“Tonight is not theatrical,” Lorenzo said quietly as we took our table. “It is inventory. Who’s loyal. Who’s hungry. Who thinks my corridor can be rented by the month.”


“Who thinks your woman can be,” Matteo added, eyes glittering.


I turned my head just enough. “Try me.”


Dante’s mouth almost smiled. “She will do fine.”


Allies arrived in tidy waves: a shipping agent with a handshake too dry; two brothers from Šibenik who reeked of diesel and recent debt; a banker in a charcoal suit who smiled like a blade wrapped in velvet. Places were taken, napkins laid, false warmth poured. I let them look. Some did it furtively—the way men steal glances at saints they don’t quite believe in. Others watched too long, testing whether I was silk or wire.


Lorenzo’s hand found my knee under the linen, anchoring me to the floor and the story at once. He didn’t have to look for me; he always knew where I was. “If they address you,” he said, voice hardly breath, “you answer. If they test you, you cut.”


“I remember the lesson,” I said.


“Good.” His thumb traced a slow stroke that said both mine and live.


Iliopoulos didn’t come in person, of course. He sent a man who smelled like cologne and old money, with a square jaw that belonged to someone else’s grandfather. He introduced himself as Themistoklis like he meant for the syllables to do half his work.


“Don Bellucci,” he said, and bowed exactly enough. “My employer regrets he cannot attend. He is, however, very interested in… etiquette.”


“Good,” Lorenzo said. “My etiquette is expensive.”


There were toasts. There were oysters. There was fish with lemon and tiresome wine. There was talk of weather and football and supply chains said like prayers. Beneath it, the real conversation slid like a current: routes, risk, what percentage of what and how often. We said little; we let them talk. People confess to silence faster than to priests.


The trouble walked in late wearing a Croatian leather jacket and Petrović’s fear on his face. A courier again—different boy, same breed. He conferred with the host, looked toward our table, and made the mistake of letting his gaze snag on me.


Rocco rose with mountain patience. “No,” he told the room, an answer to a question nobody admitted to asking. The courier swallowed his errand and retreated behind the glass.


Themistoklis watched with the small smile of a man who brought a match to a fireworks stand. “We hope to avoid drama,” he said.


“I live where drama comes to rest,” Lorenzo said mildly. “Bring your business and your respect, and you will leave with both hands.”


The banker in charcoal cleared his throat. “There is, of course, the matter of Split,” he said, eyebrows doing more work than his courage. “Three containers. An unfortunate clerical… oversight.”


Dante placed a folded sheet by his plate and did not unfold it. “Oversights don’t buy university tuitions.”


A Šibenik brother decided to be brave. “Perhaps it is simpler. Perhaps we speak of mistakes, and then no more.”


“Perhaps,” Lorenzo agreed, then let the word hang. “But mistakes become tradition if nobody bleeds.”


Silence hit the table. Themistoklis raised his glass. “Then let us agree no one here should bleed tonight.”


Matteo’s grin was all invitation. “Depends who counts as here.”


He wanted a spark. He wanted the air to catch. I felt the men around us lean toward a fight the way plants turn toward sun. Naples had trained me to hear the moment when talking turns thin. Split sounded the same.


I put my hand over Lorenzo’s—the hand under the linen bound to my knee. I didn’t squeeze. I simply existed where he did, a fact the room would have to work around.


“You’re right,” I said to Themistoklis, my voice smooth enough to pass for grace. “No bleeding. Etiquette is a beautiful word. In my husband’s house, it means three things: you pay what you owe, you protect what’s yours, and you don’t touch what belongs to someone else.”


Eyes tracked to Lorenzo’s hand. He didn’t remove it. He let them translate.


“Split will balance,” I continued. “The route will be clean. And you will not bring other men’s brokers to our table again.”


“Are you sure you want to speak for him?” Themistoklis asked lightly, testing the fence for weak boards. “It is a heavy thing, to speak for a man like your husband.”


I smiled. “I don’t speak for him. I speak with him.” I turned my face enough to catch Lorenzo’s profile, all hewn lines and a mouth that could ruin cities. “Isn’t that right?”


He didn’t look away from the Greek. “Always,” he said, and the word was a blade sliding back into its scabbard.


A waiter arrived to refill glasses with the solemnity of a doctor. As he leaned, his cuff rode up, far enough for me to see the thin ink curl behind his wristwatch. Not Croatian. Not Greek. Cyrillic. Братство. Brotherhood.


I didn’t touch Lorenzo or tilt my head. I let my breath change—one beat, two—and that was enough. His fingers tightened once on my knee before the hand disappeared under the tablecloth, signaling across the room to men who could read the invisible.


Rocco shifted. Dante looked at the window’s reflection instead of the window itself. Matteo’s grin went honest for the first time all night.


“Forgive me,” I told the waiter, the way one speaks to bees. “The lemon is beautiful tonight.”


He didn’t answer. His eyes, on the way down, lost their interest in plates and found too much interest in exits.


“Bring two more,” Lorenzo said softly, and the waiter almost bowed to the wrong master.


When he left, Themistoklis smiled without showing teeth. “Split has such… passionate service.”


“Split has such hungry men,” Lorenzo said. “The hungry steal bread until someone makes them prefer hunger. We are here to make preferences.”


The Šibenik brothers laughed too loudly. The banker reached for his glass and didn’t drink. Outside the glass wall, the courier paced and pretended not to. Inside, the music changed song and kept its heartbeat.


Dante unfolded his paper. Photographs spilled like cards. The foreman from Iliopoulos’s yard laughing with the Dubrovnik broker; a van’s open mouth; a crate’s skin half-peeled to show its real name underneath. The proof arranged itself, quiet and devastating.


“Enough,” Themistoklis said pleasantly, but his eyes had stopped smiling. “We see. We pay. We keep the corridor neat.”


“You also keep your men neat,” Lorenzo said. “I don’t want your waiter bleeding in my soup.”


The Greek’s gaze flicked, quick and sharp, to the kitchen door the not-waiter had just taken. Calculation moved behind his eyes like a tide shifting its mind. He lifted his glass.


“To etiquette,” he said.


We drank. The decision fell into the room like a stone dropped down a well. You could feel when it struck bottom.


After that, language grew easy. Numbers remembered their manners. The banker mentioned insurance; Dante mentioned margins; the Šibenik brothers decided they had always believed in cleaning corridors. The courier outside fled whatever message he had brought and would learn what kind of men still expected him to deliver it.


By dessert, Split was civilized again.


By the time we stood, the men at the other tables had already decided what story they would carry home. That the Don had visited the sea and the sea had apologized. That the woman at his side had teeth.


We stepped into the night like we owned it. The harbor lights blinked, and the wind brought the kind of cold that promises weather. Rocco peeled off left with two men, shadows scouting other shadows. Dante’s phone hummed.


“Kitchen door is clean now,” he said without looking up. “Our friend has changed shifts permanently.”


“Send flowers,” Matteo said.


“Don’t,” Lorenzo answered. “Save the roses for the living.”


We were almost to the cars when the world tried to take a bite.


It came thin and fast from the dark between two parked vans—a glint, a rush, a hand that expected fear to be slower than steel. There wasn’t time for thought. My body remembered the corridor in Naples, the wrong-moving shadow, the radio crack. I moved.


I caught the wrist the way Rocco taught the guards to, turning my shoulder and my weight into leverage, driving the man’s arm down so the blade met stone. His breath went out in a grunt. He tried to pivot. Dante’s foot took his knee. The knife skittered; Rocco’s hand arrived like a wall. Matteo laughed, delighted and dark.


The second man chose badly and went for me instead of running. I didn’t yell. I broke his attention the only way I had without weapons—I stepped into him, too close for his arm to work, and brought my knee up where men learn lessons. He folded with a sound like a prayer ripped in half.


Lorenzo didn’t say my name. He was already there, already heat and iron, already the quiet that falls after thunder. His hand closed on the first attacker’s throat—not to crush, just to teach. His eyes were winter.


“Who opened the door for you?” he asked in Croatian as neat as his Italian, as if he’d been born at the edge of every sea.


The man tried bravado and spit blood on his own shoe. “Everyone opens for money.”


“Then you paid the wrong currency,” Lorenzo said gently. He loosened his grip a fraction and let the man breathe exactly enough. “Run home. Tell your employer we don’t tip.”


He released him. The man stumbled, shocked to be alive. He ran. The second crawled, one hand between his legs, leaving behind pride and a small stain the sea would ignore by morning.


I stood very still because my legs wanted to shake and I wouldn’t let them. When I finally turned my head, Lorenzo was already watching me the way men watch the first fire they didn’t start.


“Don’t ask if I’m all right,” I told him.


“I wasn’t going to,” he said. “I was going to tell you you were beautiful.”


That nearly did what the knife couldn’t.


He took my hand and lifted it, pressed his mouth to the inside of my wrist where the pulse had learned his name. “Home,” he said to the others without looking away from me.


“Hotel,” Dante corrected, practical even with adrenaline singing. “Then arrangements.”


“Then arrangements,” Lorenzo agreed.


Matteo walked backward a few steps, watching me like I had reinvented a game he wanted to play. “Our principessa learns fast.”


“She was always like this,” Rocco said. “Now you’re just paying attention.”


The suite faced the harbor. Night smudged the horizon; boats hummed in their sleep. We were alone as a cathedral after mass.


He locked the door because he knew I liked the sound. I leaned back against it and the wood learned the shape of my spine.


“You didn’t flinch,” he said.


“I like the part where they run.”


His smile was barely there. “You like the part where you win.”


He crossed the room without hurry, the way storms cross maps: inevitable, precise. When he reached me, I felt the static of the evening rise between our skins, the echo of a tableful of men and a knife in the dark and the way I had stepped instead of screamed.


“Say it,” I told him, breath not steady.


“You were perfect,” he said simply. “And you’re mine.”


“Always.”


The heat between us didn’t climb; it arrived, already at altitude. He didn’t bother with distance. His hands found my waist and my jaw, and his mouth found mine like a man returning to oxygen. The kiss was not polite; it never was. It tasted like salt and victory and the relief we never admitted in public.


“Look at me,” he said, voice low, and I did. “I want you loud.”


“You have me,” I answered, and the rest of the night forgot it had edges.


He lifted me and carried me through the dimness, setting me where he wanted me—where the city lay in the glass and the bed took my weight and his palm rode my thigh in a claim I craved like absolution. He spoke in the power I wore like silk and steel: mine and here and don’t look away. I didn’t. I met him with everything I had—hunger, loyalty, the fierce joy of surviving together—and let the world narrow to breath and heat and vow.


He shifted, trapping my leg beneath his weight, and tore away the final barrier with a desperate noise caught in his throat. "Tell me what I do to you," he demanded, burying himself in a single, unhurried, agonizing stroke that felt like he was rearranging my bones. I gasped, the sound ragged, and arched into the deep invasion. His mouth found my ear, whispering the things he needed to own: "Mine. Here. Deep."


He held my gaze, forcing me to watch the power and the terror etched on his face as he began a deep, punishing rhythm—a slow, deliberate grind that stretched and filled me completely. The friction was a furious fire, a physical manifestation of his possessiveness, and I met him thrust for thrust, clinging to the edge of the bed, moaning his name until the world dissolved into the wet, frantic sound of our bodies.


When the shatter came, it was a quiet catastrophe—my body breaking open on his name, his body answering with the kind of surrender men like him never show anyone else. He held me through it, forehead pressed to mine, breath shaking like a confession.


“Nobody touches you,” he said against my mouth.


“They can try,” I whispered. “It won’t end how they think.”


He made a sound that might have been a laugh if it weren’t worship. He folded me into him and the night set us like stones.


After, he lay on his back and stared at the ceiling the way men do when they’re listening to wars only they can hear. I turned on my side and traced the cut of his ribs, the bruise at his bicep, the pulse at his throat. The harbor beat time against the glass. Split pretended to sleep.


“Tomorrow?” I asked.


“Tomorrow,” he said. “We close the foreman. We buy the server. We cut a corridor. We make the Greek say please.”


“And Karras?”


“He will come to the table when he is hungry,” Lorenzo said. “Tonight, we fed him fear.”


I put my hand over his heart. He covered it with his and pressed down, sealing something neither of us had words for.


“I love you,” he said, softer than the sea.


“I love you too,” I said and meant it with every breath within me.


He slept. I didn’t. I watched the harbor and felt the city test the edges of us. People think love softens men like him. It doesn’t. It makes them exact. It makes them dangerous with purpose.


At four a.m., Dante’s message lit the room briefly blue.


— Server secured. Foreman flipped.


— Themistoklis requests breakfast. Says Iliopoulos prefers humility with coffee.


I smiled into the dark. Naples had taught me the price of quiet. Split had just shown me its profit.


I slid closer to Lorenzo and let the dawn find us the way it always did in this life—at the edge of another agreement, the taste of salt on the air, the promise of a day that would demand we pay in something we hadn’t meant to spend.


So be it. We would count. We would cut. We would collect.


And if the fire finally reached us, we would teach it our names.

Chapter 9

A Kingdom of Salt


Lorenzo


Midnight in Split tasted like iron.


The harbor kept humming—boats snoring, gulls arguing with the dark—while my phone pulsed twice against my palm. Dante’s signal. The kind that meant move.


I dressed without waking her. Faith slept on her side facing the window, hair spilled like ink, mouth soft in a way I would kill to protect and had just proven I would. I let the back of my knuckles learn her cheek for a second, then I left because I was the man who had to.


Rocco waited at the lift with a jacket in his hands and a question under his tongue. “Car?”


“Two,” I said. “No lights.”


By the time the lobby sighed us out into the cold, Dante’s second text arrived.


— Foreman at the yard. Broker at a bar called Vatra. Choose.


“Both,” I told him on the call. “You take Vatra. I’ll take the yard.”


“Matteo?” Dante asked.


“In my pocket,” I lied.


I hung up and pocketed the lie.


The road to the yard cut through a shiver of warehouses, their tin skins shining like old scars. Fog rose from the water and laid itself across the ground in veils. We ghosted the gate—my men in plain coats, hands empty until they weren’t. The Iliopoulos yard looked half-asleep; half-asleep is when people dream they’re safe.


“Two on the gantry,” Rocco murmured. “One by the shack.”


“Take the gantry first,” I said. “Quiet.”


He moved like a rumor, up the ladder and into the fog. Below, I crossed to the shack and opened the door as if I belonged to the hinges. The guard jerked, just once, then saw my face and decided breathing was more important than heroism. He put his hands on the desk and looked at a spot beyond my shoulder like perhaps mercy lived there.


“Water,” I said, reading his sweat. “And a telephone.”


He offered both. I didn’t drink. I didn’t call.


The foreman was in the container canyon—dark coat, bare head, a cigarette flaring and dying, flaring and dying, the rhythm of a man who dared his own fear to say something useful. He heard our steps, turned, and set his jaw the way men do when they plan to perform bravery for three minutes and then run.


“Good evening,” he said in Greek. “You’re trespassing.”


“I’m something else,” I said in the same language and let him choose which.


His eyes flicked left toward the port, right toward the water, up toward the gantry where my men no longer had silhouettes. He found no doors.


“You had a ring made,” I said. “Congratulations. It’s a pity diamonds don’t float.”


He tried a laugh. It failed. “You can’t touch me here.”


“You already touched yourself,” I said. “When you sold a corridor you didn’t own.”


His chin came up like a buoy. “Business is business.”


“Numbers are numbers,” I corrected. “And yours are wrong.”


Rocco stepped from the fog and the foreman’s courage learned the weight of reality. I nodded at a stack of pallets. We walked him there without scaring the night.


“Here is my arithmetic,” I told him. “Twelve boxes. Nine arrive. Three go on holiday. You are paid by one man for the theft, by another to pretend not to notice. Here is your mistake: you left the solvent in the air. Here is your second mistake: you bought a ring instead of buying a road out of the city.”


He shrugged, jaw tight. “You cannot prove anything.”


“I don’t have to,” I said. “I came for something simpler than proof.”


“What?”


“Names.”


He swallowed. The cigarette shook. “It’s just a job.”


“Tell that to the boy we put in the ground,” I said, and watched understanding hit him like surf.


He stared at me then—not the suit, not the story, me. Men recognize weather when it stands close enough. “If I give you names,” he asked, “do I live?”


“Yes,” I said.


Matteo stepped out of the fog then, smile bright enough to be a mistake. He flipped his knife open and close, the click-click a heartbeat no one had asked for. “Or we let him live with fewer parts,” he purred. “A compromise.”


I didn’t look at him. I didn’t have to. “Rocco,” I said, and the big man moved to stand between my brother and the foreman like a wall that loved its job.


“Names,” I repeated.


The foreman stared at the knife, then at Rocco’s hands, then at me. Men make choices in three places—fear, pride, and poverty. Pride had gotten him this far. Fear would get him home.


“Petrović was petty cash,” he said, voice thin. “He handled drivers and police who like wine. The broker is called Davor. He works out of Dubrovnik and uses a cousin in Split to switch crates. We repaint labels here and mark the hinges there.” His mouth twisted. “Your man at the port is clean. Your cameras are not.”


“Davor,” I repeated. “And above him?”


He hesitated. I pushed, not with hands. With time.


“Above him,” he said finally, in a rush, “is Iliopoulos some days. And a German other days. But the one who thinks he is a king? Karras. He is laughing because he thinks your family is busy fighting itself.”


Matteo’s coin died in his palm.


“Addresses,” I said.


He gave them. Dante would verify. The math would hold.


“You live,” I told him, and meant it. Mercy is an investment when you make it in the right account.


Matteo leaned into the line of air between us, tempting the fact. “We should take a finger to discourage future arithmetic.”


“We will take his margin instead,” I said.


The foreman’s shoulders sagged. He creased like paper that had been folded too many times. “If I lose my job,” he ventured, “I lose everything.”


“You lost your job when your courage rented itself,” I said. “Run. Keep the ring. Move to a city where nobody knows how you paid for it.”


He ran. It wasn’t graceful. It didn’t have to be. Rocco watched him vanish into fog, then turned the question he hadn’t asked into a look.


“You made a promise,” he said.


“I keep them when I mean them,” I answered. “That’s why they work.”


The yard breathed around us—metal settling, cables ticking in the damp. The shack radio whispered a station without words. Above us, the fog took the gantry in its arms.


“Dante?” I asked into my phone.


“Vatra is an ugly room,” he said. “The broker is uglier. He likes to feel important. I made him feel important. He gave us the server access and a name he didn’t mean to say.”


“Whose?”


“Karras’s Englishman,” Dante said. “The one who signs at banks. He wants to meet.”


“Of course he does,” I said. “People who launder think they are clever because their hands look clean.”


“Shall I schedule something charming?” Dante asked.


“Tomorrow,” I said. “Public place. Too many cameras. Too much noise.”


“Your brother?” There was a sheet of iron under the words.


“In my pocket,” I lied again.


We took the long road back along the sea because it was the only honest thing in Split. The water shouldered the rocks as if it meant to wear them down in a century and had decided to start tonight. The cars hummed. The men behind me pretended to be furniture.


Matteo crackled with unshed fuel. “We should have taken more than names,” he said after the third kilometer. “You had him.”


“I still have him,” I said. “He’ll carry my message farther alive.”


“Bodies carry messages farther,” he said.


“Bodies invite lights,” I said. “Lights invite Rome.”


“Rome, Rome, Rome,” he sang softly, childish and sharp. “Someday I’ll light a fire and let Rome read by it.”


I looked at him then, at the boy who had grown into a grin he didn’t know how to put down, at the man who loved a stage more than a throne. “If you burn the house, there is no crown to keep warm.”


He smiled like I had joked. I hadn’t.


The hotel lifted out of the fog like a ship decided to stop pretending. The lobby was gold, the way expensive places are at night. The lift doors closed on our reflection—three men in a box, all wearing the same last name differently.


At my floor I said, “Go with Rocco. Trade the cars. Shuffle the plates. Lock down the service entrance.”


“Mother?” he asked, mocking, and then shrugged at my stare. “Fine. I’ll make it neat.”


“Neat is not enough,” I said. “Make it invisible.”


When the doors opened, my anger wanted to follow him. My discipline turned it around and led it down the hall.


Faith opened the suite before my key found the slot. Of course she did. She’d been listening to the carpet and the hinges and the gravity in my tread. The room behind her smelled like the sea had decided to be warm for once.


“Blood?” she asked quietly.


“Only on the ledger,” I said. “For now.”


She scanned my face the way the city scanned the horizon. What she looked for I’m not sure—cracks, smoke. She stepped aside, let me pass, then closed the door and locked it because she knew I liked the sound.


“Tell me,” she said.


So I did. I told her about the foreman’s ring and his fear and the way a name buys you more than a finger sometimes. I told her about Davor at Vatra and Karras’s Englishman with his clean hands and his dirty accounts. I told her about Matteo’s itch and my patience and how both were problems that could be turned into tools if I remembered which was which.


When I finished, she came close enough that I stopped talking. She touched the bruise on my bicep with her mouth, not as sympathy—as witness. Then she lifted her face, and the study of it did something to the space behind my ribs I have never had the courage to name.


“You didn’t wake me,” she said.


“You needed sleep.”


“You needed me.”


“I always do,” I said, and that was the rawest truth I allowed anyone.


Her hands rose to my shirt. She undid the top button, then the next, slow enough to count, quick enough to prove she had decided the counting would stop when she said so. The heat between us was not frantic. It was the kind that arrives after people survive something and choose each other a second time.


“Look at me,” I told her.


She did. She always did.


The night bent around us until the room forgot it had walls. I carried her to the glass where the harbor threw its cheap stars, and she took what I offered and gave back what I couldn’t ask for out loud. When we were quiet again, she lay with her ear over my heart and let her fingertips draw maps against my skin only she would ever read.


“Matteo,” she said into silence, a word like a match.


“Matteo,” I agreed.


“You can’t keep him out of the wind,” she said. “He was born facing it.”


“I can break him if I have to,” I said, because there was mercy in not lying to her.


She lifted her head and stared at me until I felt judged in a way I would accept only from her. “Break him and you’ll bury more than a brother. You’ll bury the part of yourself that still believes in blood.”


“The part that believes in you survived worse,” I said.


“Then keep that part alive.”


A small, crooked smile found me. “You are not soft, Faith.”


“I’m your lens,” she said. “Use me.”


I slept for an hour. Maybe two. A knock I didn’t like woke me, the nylon scratch of the do-not-disturb tag tapping wood. Rocco’s voice came through the door, low: “Boss.”


I moved without waking her this time. I opened the door a hand’s width. His face was like granite.


“What.”


“Matteo didn’t go to his room,” he said. “He took two boys and a car we didn’t assign. Cameras show him near Vatra.”


Dante’s ugly bar. The broker. I felt a clean, cold thread pull taut under my breastbone. “When?”


“Twenty minutes ago.”


I looked back at the bed. Faith slept on, trusting a house she didn’t know, trusting me in ways that made a man choose his better self.


“Keep her here,” I told Rocco. “In the room, if you can. If she insists, in the hall. But she doesn’t read the night unless I say so.”


“You’re going to Vatra,” he said.


I didn’t answer because it wasn’t a question. He fell in at my shoulder and the lift delivered us to the kind of lobby that likes to pretend bad things happen elsewhere.


Outside, Split had sharpened again. Fog thinned to the kind of film knives make. We cut through it. Vatra glowed like a wound—neon script, scarlet door, the damp stink of liars.


Dante sat on the curb with his elbows on his knees and his phone in his hand. He stood when he saw me and put the phone away as if I were the better tool.


“Inside,” he said. “Your brother is writing slogans again.”


“What did he do.”


“Tried to buy a confession with showmanship.” Dante’s mouth was a line. “The broker is on his knees. Not metaphor.”


I went in. The music thought it was winning. The room did not.


Matteo had Davor by the collar, jacket twisted into a choke, knee pinning the man’s spine to the sticky floor. Two of Matteo’s borrowed boys held a third man against the wall. The rest of the bar had decided that floors were interesting.


“I asked nicely,” Matteo said without looking up. “He didn’t like my manners.”


Davor bled from the lip, from one ear, from the place on his pride where men leak first. He saw me and did the math. “Don—”


“Quiet,” I said, and the room obeyed.


I took one step toward my brother and saw it, at last, the hairline fracture I had been pretending belonged to the building: Matteo’s smile had begun to look like a wound he made himself.


“Up,” I told him. “Now.”


He laughed and stood and let Davor breathe. The broker crawled toward a table and the table moved. He had nowhere to go and everywhere to watch.


“You told me to keep Naples loud,” Matteo said. “I thought I’d try it in Croatian.”


“I told you to keep Naples,” I said. “I didn’t tell you to import my signature.”


He spread his hands in a saint’s parody. “Look around. Everyone will carry this story home. It helps.”


“It helps Karras,” Dante said from the door. “He wanted the room loud.”


I nodded once. “Go,” I told the borrowed boys, and they went. “Rocco,” I said, and my man went to the bartender, left money he wouldn’t count, and told him to forget English for a night.


Matteo rolled his shoulders like a fighter about to pretend he was in a ring. “You can’t keep them afraid with silence,” he said. “They need theater.”


“They need arithmetic,” I said. “They need a ledger that always comes out the same.”


He stepped closer, eyes bright with something I recognized and did not want to, because if I recognized it I had to admit it had once lived in me. “You are in love,” he said softly, viciously. “You are building a church inside a war.”


“Yes,” I said. “And I know how to preach.”


He blinked. He hadn’t expected yes.


I turned to Davor. “Names,” I said. “Accounts. Times. The Englishman’s face.”


He gave them. Men like him always do when the room’s gravity changes owners. Dante wrote without looking down. Rocco watched the doors breathe. Matteo watched me, not the broker, and that was the problem.


When we had enough to cut a corridor and a throat, I put my hand on my brother’s shoulder. He tensed the way a boy does when he expects the belt and hopes for the hand.


“Walk,” I said.


We went out together into the kind of night that steals heat from the mouth. Dante took Davor to a car that wasn’t ours but thought it was. Rocco stood ten paces off and pretended stone.


At the curb I let the silence learn the shape of what I was going to put inside it. Matteo waited, proud and reckless and mine.


“Listen to me,” I said. “You want sky. I know. You want the city to sing your name. I know. But this is not a song. It is math. And if you keep moving when I tell you to be still, I will cut you from the equation.”


“You’d kill me,” he said, testing whether the word would break his teeth.


“I would bury the part of me that loves you,” I said, and felt each syllable open something ugly in my chest. “And then I would live with the man who killed it.”


For a heartbeat, his face was my face when we were fifteen, when our father taught us consequence with his hands and we learned not to make him do the work twice. Then the grin came back, but less certain, like a curtain caught in a draft.


“I’m not your enemy,” he said.


“You’re the man who could become him,” I said. “Don’t.”


He stared at me. Then he laughed like a boy again, small and mean. “Faith is changing you.”


“She is making me exact,” I said. “Pray I stay that way.”


He looked away first. He always had. “Fine. Do the numbers. I’ll do the pressure.”


“You’ll do nothing I haven’t asked you to,” I said, and the night believed me because I believed me.


We went back to the hotel, the city peeling off us like skin that didn’t fit anymore. On the way up, Rocco’s phone whispered a message to his palm.


“Englishman confirmed,” he said. “Nine a.m. By the ferry.”


“Good,” I said. “Bring coffee and men who look like tourists.”


In the suite, Faith stood by the glass in a robe that made linen look like something rich. Her eyes found mine; the question didn’t bother pretending it wasn’t there.


“Handled,” I said.


She searched my face again. “For tonight,” she translated.


“For tonight,” I agreed, and let myself come apart exactly as much as the hour would allow: just enough to take her mouth, to set my hands on her waist, to put my forehead to hers and borrow the heat she carried like a shield.


“Stay,” I asked without shame.


“I wasn’t leaving,” she said. Her thumb stroked the line between my brows until it smoothed. “Tomorrow we count again.”


“We count,” I said. “And we collect.”


Dawn would come bleeding over the ferries soon enough. The Englishman would discover humility with coffee. The foreman would buy a train ticket. Davor would learn that a ledger could be merciful and still be a weapon. Matteo would either obey or make me finish the sentence I had started tonight.


I slid into the bed beside the only vow I had never regretted and let the city say what cities say when they know who owns their dark: for now. For now was enough. For now was blood without bodies. For now, the kingdom of salt was ours.
