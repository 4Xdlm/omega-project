Chapter 9

The Woman They Didn’t Expect

Sophie

Isit on my porch swing, still in the clothes I wore earlier, hands wrapped tightly around a mug of now-cold tea. The breeze has a bite to it tonight, but I don’t move. Not even to go inside. My heart hasn’t settled since I left Ethan’s house.


I walked into a storm today. And for a moment, I was the eye of it.


Mia had been on the floor, screaming, lost in a world of sound and fear that no one could reach. No one except me. I didn’t think. I just acted. The second her hands reached for mine, something in me cracked open. And when her sobs faded in my arms, when her breathing calmed against my shoulder, I knew.


I’m in too deep.


Caroline Ford had stared at me like I was a puzzle piece jammed into the wrong spot. Like I didn’t belong. And William didn’t raise his voice or throw around judgment. He didn’t have to. His questions were laced with concern that barely hid the doubt underneath.

And Caroline, her voice cold and her words sharper than I expected, had said, “I don’t want anything to do with her or her reputation.”


Then came the final blow: “She was involved in a scandal."


I hadn’t meant to overhear them. I had quietly slipped back inside to grab my jacket, hoping to leave without drawing attention. But as I stepped through the hallway and neared the kitchen, the sound of their voices stopped me cold. That’s when I heard them, just through the slightly ajar kitchen window. Their voices were low but not low enough. I stood there, frozen, as every word landed like a stone in my chest.


A risk. That’s what I am.


The porch creaks softly beneath me as I shift, the sun slipping lower, setting the sky in hues of amber and soft blue. I should change out of these clothes. I should pour the tea down the sink and move on with my evening.


But I can’t.


Then, there's the knock on my door, soft, almost hesitant.

“I just… needed to say thank you,” he says, voice low and rough.

He doesn’t enter. He doesn’t even glance past me into the house. He just stands there, breathing like each word costs him something.


“You’ve done more for Mia than anyone else,” he says. “Including me.”


His voice falters, and for a second, I see it. Not the guarded man he usually is, but the one underneath. The one that still blames himself for things he can’t control.

He doesn’t finish. He doesn’t need to.

His eyes meet mine.

He nods, almost like he expected that.


“I know,” he says. “But somehow… you’re still holding us together.”


We both stand there in the quiet. The world soft around us.


He looks like he wants to say more. Like he might step closer.

He rubs the back of his neck. “Just… don’t forget you matter here too. It’s not just Mia.”


His words hit harder than I expect. And before I can reply, he nods and turns to go.


"Goodnight, Sophie."


"Goodnight, Ethan."

1 new email: Oakridge Elementary

Because once, I believed I was doing the right thing. And it still cost me everything.


A car passes by slowly. Somewhere down the street, a dog barks. The town is quiet again, the way small towns always are when the sun finally disappears.

What if they’re right? What if I really am the risk? The reminder of a failure I can’t outrun. I want to believe I’m here because I belong, because Mia needs me, because Ethan sees something in me worth trusting. But doubt clings to me like the headlines never stopped.

I don’t know what this means. I don’t know what comes next.


But I know how I feel.


I care about them. Deeply. Desperately.


And that’s what scares me the most.


Because I’m not just a visitor anymore.


And maybe, just maybe, I never was.

"You planning to fall into my yard every day or just on holidays?"


She smirks. "Guess you’ll have to wait and find out."


But before I can respond, she turns on her heel and walks back to her porch, sunhat in hand, her shoulders straighter than before.


—


As the afternoon dips toward evening, I’m in the garage, pretending to fix a squeaky hinge on Mia’s toy chest just to stay busy.


The sound of metal and wood is better than the silence.


By day, I run a small but growing security software company based right here in Oakridge. At least, I used to. Ever since Mia came into my care, the business has been hanging by a thread.


Investor calls get missed. Updates fall behind schedule. Most days I feel like I’m barely keeping the roof above us.


I’m not just a tech entrepreneur anymore. I’m a full-time guardian, a single man raising a child with needs bigger than my confidence and fears louder than my instincts.


I’ve been estranged from my parents for years, and half the time I wonder if I’m doing any of this right.


My life’s become one long line of sacrifices: school pickups, therapy appointments, late-night debugging sessions. No room left for things like vulnerability, or softness, or even asking for help.


Mia Ford, my six-year-old niece, is upstairs asleep. Bright, curious, and full of questions, she’s been living with me ever since the accident that took her parents—my younger brother Andrew and his wife Rachel.


Mia had a hard day earlier. She struggled with her reading that morning and didn’t want help. I didn’t push.


I never push.


I just wish I knew how to reach her.


The truth is, I’m winging this parenting thing. Badly.


I miss investor calls. I forget to charge her tablet. I burn frozen waffles.


I’m not cut out for this. But I’m the only one she has.


And somehow, when Sophie’s voice floated across the yard earlier, for a second, it didn’t feel so heavy. Like maybe the world wasn’t just weight pressing down on my chest.


I set the screwdriver down and wipe my palms on my jeans.


I shouldn’t care that she’s back. She’s Luke’s sister. She was off-limits then. She still is now.


But something about her has changed. Or maybe I’m just seeing her without the noise of the past.


She walks different. Straighter. Like she’s carried something heavy and finally set it down. There’s elegance in the way she moves now, like she’s comfortable in her own skin and doesn’t need anyone’s approval. Her curves are more confident, her smile more controlled, like she knows exactly who she is and dares the world to underestimate her again.


Even her voice has changed. Still warm, but sharper, wittier, with an edge that makes you want to listen carefully. It’s not just that she’s beautiful now. It’s that she knows it, and wears that confidence like armor.


I hear her laugh through the open window. It carries on the breeze like it used to in the summers when she and Luke played tag in the backyard. Only now, it sounds different. Less innocent. More... layered.


My phone buzzes.


A calendar reminder: Investor Call — Postponed Again.


Great.


Another thing slipping through the cracks. And each one chips away at my confidence. I can handle failure in business. But failing Mia? Letting her down? That’s the kind of fear that keeps me up at night, staring at the ceiling, wondering if I’m doing anything right.


I rub my eyes and stretch my back, wondering if I should even keep trying to juggle both worlds. Business and Mia. The company and fatherhood. It's like trying to hold two ropes that keep pulling in opposite directions.


The side door to the garage creaks open.


Not Mia.


Not Sophie.


"Yo!" a voice calls out. "Still working with your hands like the caveman you are?"


I look up.


Luke. Luke Taylor, Sophie’s older brother and my former best friend, the guy I used to play street hockey with until sundown and sneak Pop-Tarts from his mom’s pantry when we thought no one was looking. We built forts out of sofa cushions, dared each other to bike down Oakridge Hill without brakes, and once got grounded for an entire month after turning his dad’s tool shed into a haunted house for Halloween. Those were the days we thought nothing could break us.


Standing in the doorway, basketball tucked under one arm, grin lazy as ever.


He hasn’t changed much. Still tall, still athletic, still thinks everything can be solved with a pickup game.


I wipe my hands on a rag and nod toward the porch. "Didn’t think you’d show up."


"Didn’t think you’d still be hiding out." He smirks. "Come on. I’m starting a game over at Jackson’s court. You in?"


I hesitate. It’s been a while since I played. Feels like another lifetime.


Still… something in me says yes before I can stop it. "Yeah. Sure."


We step out onto the porch, joking about his weak layups and my rusty jump shot. It almost feels normal.


The air is cooler now, streaked with the orange glow of a setting sun. Crickets have started their nightly chorus, and the shadows across the lawn stretch long and lean. A light breeze carries the scent of cut grass and something sugary from the bakery down the block, cinnamon maybe, or peach cobbler. It’s the kind of late summer evening Oakridge does best. Quiet. Predictable. Safe.


And then the hose clicks on.


We both turn.


There she is.


Sophie.


Wearing a soft t-shirt, hair tied up, watering her mother’s petunias like she’s been here the whole time.


Luke stiffens.


His smile dies.


"Sophie?"


She looks up.


Freezes.


Then forces a casual smile. "Hey, Luke."


His mouth opens. Closes.


His eyes shift from her… to me.


"You knew."


It’s not a question.


My stomach sinks.


"Luke, I—"


But it’s too late.


The weight of old wounds crashes down between us.


Again.

“I didn’t know she was back until a few hours ago,” Ethan says, voice low and careful. His gaze flicks briefly to me, then to the ground. “I was as surprised as you are.”


Luke’s eyes narrow. “You expect me to believe that?”

“But you didn’t tell me either.” Luke drops the basketball with a dull thud. “You kept it quiet. On purpose.”

“I was,” I say quickly. My hands fidget at my sides. “I just... I hadn’t figured out how.”


His face falls. Not angry now, just hurt. His shoulders slump. “I’m your brother, Soph. You don’t think I’d want to know you were back? Maybe I’d want to help?”


The guilt comes fast and sharp. “I didn’t mean to keep it from you.”


He shakes his head slowly, like the pieces are falling into place and none of them are good.


And just like that, the old wound opens again.


“You know,” Luke says, his voice quiet but hard, “I thought we buried this years ago. I thought the past was done.”


Ethan doesn’t reply. His face is unreadable, jaw locked, lips drawn tight.


But the silence is telling.

“Doesn’t it?” Luke throws up his hands. “Because from where I’m standing, it looks like we’re right back where we were.”


“We’re not,” Ethan says, jaw tight. “Things are different now.”

His back is rigid, shoulders squared, but the way his hand clenches at his side betrays just how deep the disappointment runs. I let out a dry laugh. “Trust me, I’m not. Whatever crush I had? Long gone. Like your social skills.”


He opens his mouth but I cut him off. “And by the way, Luke didn’t blow up because he’s dramatic. He blew up because for years you acted like I was some awkward mistake you had to avoid.”


His face softens just slightly. “That’s not what it was.”


“Could’ve fooled me,” I say, crossing my arms. “You were like a robot anytime I came near you.”


“I didn’t know what to do,” he mutters.


“You could’ve started with basic human interaction,” I say. “A ‘hey, thanks for the muffins, kid’ wouldn’t have killed you.”


He huffs. “You’re still the same smart mouth.”


“Yeah, well, now it comes with boundaries and better hair,” I shoot back.

It started with a tray of still-warm brownies and a pair of hands that wouldn’t stop shaking. I tried to smile. “I thought you might be hungry.”

Didn’t say a word.

Ethan was still out there, by the fire, kissing her like she was the only girl who ever existed.

And Luke doesn’t forgive people who treat his family like they’re disposable.


Not even if they were once his best friend.

He nods slowly, like he’s finally seeing me. “I can tell.”


Ethan takes a step closer but stops. His voice is quieter now. “I never forgot about you.”


I glance at him this time, just enough to meet his eyes. “You were part of a time in my life I’ve grown past. That chapter is over, not because I’ve been clinging to anger, but because I’ve moved forward. What happened back then? It shaped me, sure. But it doesn’t define me anymore.”

“I didn’t know how to handle it back then,” he says, his tone low. “You were this bright, innocent thing, and I thought pushing you away was the only way to protect you.”


“Protect me?” I raise an eyebrow. “You could’ve just been a decent human and said, ‘Hey, not interested.’ Instead, you acted like I was a potted plant and you kept forgetting to water.”


He lets out a short breath that could almost be a laugh, but there’s guilt in his eyes.


“I get it,” I say, lifting one shoulder. “We were both figuring things out in our own messed-up ways. You shut down, I dressed up my hope with glitter pens and daydreams no one asked for.”


He nods once. “I didn’t mean to make you feel invisible.”


“Maybe not,” I say, voice light but pointed. “But you did. And the worst part? I let it get to me for way too long. But not anymore.”


He takes a step forward, but I lift a hand slightly, stopping him. “I’m not that girl anymore, Ethan. I don’t need saving. I don’t need you to validate anything about me. I’m the one who closes chapters now, and guess what? I’m really good at it.”


We stand in that quiet, awkward stillness. The air feels thick, like the space between us is holding too many things we can’t say. It’s not romantic or warm, it’s just heavy. Like we’re standing at the edge of a conversation neither of us is brave enough to finish.


But then…


“Dad?”


The voice is soft. Small. A girl’s voice.


I stop mid-step.


I turn around slowly.


On the porch stands a little girl, dark curls, sleepy eyes, a worn stuffed elephant in one hand. She’s in pajamas, one sock sliding off her foot, and she’s rubbing her eyes with a tiny fist.


My heart stumbles.


She looks up at Ethan, voice groggy. “Daddy, I had a bad dream.”


Ethan’s whole body changes. The tension melts from his shoulders as he steps toward her. His expression softens completely. “Hey, sweetheart. It’s okay. I’m here.”


Dad.


The word repeats in my head like an echo.


Ethan isn’t just playing house in Oakridge.


He’s a father.


A dad.


I feel the blood drain from my face. I thought I was past all of this. I had convinced myself that whatever I once felt was packed away with old photos and bad poetry. But something about hearing that one word, Dad, hits differently. Not because it breaks my heart. Because it confuses me. I shouldn’t care. I shouldn’t be affected. And yet… Here I am, standing frozen like someone pulled the ground out from under me.


Suddenly, everything I thought I knew about him, about us, shifts like sand under my feet.


He turns and catches the look on my face.


And for once, Ethan Ford doesn’t have a single word to say.


And me? I suddenly don’t know where to stand anymore.

Then she crouches beside Mia, careful not to crowd her. "Hey, sweet pea. I’m Sophie—the lady next door. Looks like we’ve got company, huh?"


Mia peeks out, hesitant, then turns her face back toward me.


Sophie pulls a ring of laminated cards from her tote bag. “Want to tell me how you feel this morning?”


Mia doesn’t move at first. Her eyes flick between me and Sophie. She leans further into my leg, half-hiding her face. Sophie stays still, letting the silence stretch without pressure.


“I know we haven’t met before,” Sophie says gently. “It’s okay to be unsure.”


Mia looks up at me, uncertain. I give her a reassuring nod.


Another beat passes. Then, slowly and tentatively, Mia points to a card with a blue cloud.


“Sad?” Sophie asks softly. “You want to breathe with me?”


She models a deep breath. Mia mimics her, a little shaky at first. Her small shoulders rise and fall, then again with more steadiness.


It’s not trust yet. But it’s something.


Valerie watches. Scribbles another note.


Sophie stands. Her tone shifts, confident but respectful. “Transitions are hard. But Ethan’s done everything he can to make her feel safe. And that’s more important than what’s taped to the fridge.”


Her voice is calm but steady, the kind that makes you listen. “Safety and emotional security is the foundation, right? You can teach routines. You can build structure. But you can’t teach a child to feel safe unless they already do.”


I clear my throat and add, “I know it doesn’t look perfect. But I’m trying. Every day. Mia’s been through enough change already. I’m doing everything I can to keep it steady.”


Valerie’s eyes flick to me briefly, then to Mia, still pressed against my leg. She doesn’t reply right away. There’s a small crease between her brows, like she’s considering it. But her expression gives nothing away. Finally, she closes the clipboard. “I’ll need to include all this in my report. It’s procedure.”


“Understood,” Sophie says, nodding politely.


Valerie nods stiffly, then walks to the door. “You’ll hear from us within the week.”


The door closes behind her.


Silence.


I let out a slow breath. “Well. That went well.”


Sophie gives me a look.


I rub the back of my neck. “Thanks. For stepping in.”


“You looked like you were ready to bolt,” she says, her tone lighter now, but there’s still concern in her eyes.


“I wasn’t ready,” I admit. “Not for someone with a clipboard and a checklist and a million silent judgments.”


“She was just doing her job,” Sophie replies gently.


“Yeah, well,” I mutter, sinking onto the arm of the couch, “her job might cost me everything.”


Sophie studies me. “You’re doing more right than wrong, Ethan. Mia is safe. She’s loved. That counts for more than most things on a form.”


I shake my head. “Doesn’t matter what I know. It’s what they decide. And that house? That mess? It’s not exactly screaming ‘fit guardian.’”


“She’s going to report all of that, isn’t she?” Sophie’s voice is quiet but steady.


I open my mouth, then pause. Mia’s still close, fingers curled around my pant leg. I bend down slowly and lift her into my arms. She rests her head on my shoulder with a tired sigh.


“Yeah,” I finally say. “She is.”


Sophie steps forward. “You haven’t introduced us properly.”


“Oh.” I glance between the two of them. “Mia, this is Sophie. She’s our neighbor... and a friend.”


Mia peeks up at her, shy but curious.


“And Sophie,” I say, “this is Mia.”


Mia tucks her face into my neck again, mumbling softly, “Hi, Sophie.”


Sophie’s expression softens. “Hi, Mia. It’s really nice to meet you.”


From the hallway, Miss Dana appears, holding a basket of clean laundry. She pauses when she sees us, then gives a small wave and disappears into the laundry room to give us space.


I glance back at Sophie. “Thanks again. Really. I didn’t expect you to... jump in like that.”


“You didn’t have to ask,” she says simply. “I care about what happens here. About her. About you.”


I look at her, unsure what to say. The words catch somewhere between gratitude and guilt.


“I’m not used to that,” I say finally. “People showing up.”


“Well,” she says, brushing Mia’s curls gently, “get used to it.”


Mia yawns against my shoulder. “Dad,” she mumbles, eyes half-closed. “I still want to sleep.”


Sophie’s eyes flick to mine. There’s something tender and surprised in her expression. I feel exposed, but also oddly grounded.


“I’ll let you both rest,” Sophie says softly.


I walk her to the door. As we reach it, she pauses.


“I actually came over to drop off your mail,” she says, lifting the stack still in her hand. “It landed in our box again.”


She hesitates, then adds, “Miss Dana was the one who answered the door. She looked surprised, honestly.”


“She didn’t know what was going on?” I ask.


“Not really. She told me she was just here to help around the house and take care of Mia’s needs—meals, bedtime, that kind of thing. But when she saw Mia tense up, she figured something serious was happening.”


I nod slowly. “She doesn’t have experience with autism. I think she’s trying the best she can.”


“She is,” Sophie agrees. “She told me she thought the woman at the door might be here for an inspection or something. So she stepped back and let things play out. Said she didn't want to make things worse.”


I let out a breath. “That sounds like Dana.”


“And that’s when I remembered I still had some of my old materials from teaching in New York,” Sophie continues. “Just a few things I kept in my tote in case I needed them—routine visuals, calming strategies. I figured Mia might need a way to ground herself.”


I blink, surprised. “That was Dana’s?”


Sophie lets out a soft breath. “No. That was me.”


I look at her again, this time really taking her in. “You?”


“I brought the cue cards. I had them in my tote. Leftovers from my old classroom days in New York. Didn’t think I’d ever use them again, but... I saw how overwhelmed Mia looked and I couldn’t just stand there.”


“Oh.” I take the mail from her hand, grateful for the excuse that brought her here. But I feel the awkward twist in my chest that she’d walked into the mess unannounced.


It’s not that I don’t appreciate what she did. I do. More than I can say. But there’s something about someone seeing your life in disarray. The crumbs. The laundry piles. The judgmental clipboard. It makes you want to shut the door and lock it behind you.


She must sense it.


“I wasn’t trying to intrude,” she adds gently.


“I know,” I say. “Still... thanks.”


There’s a moment where neither of us says anything. Sophie gives me a small smile, one that feels more like reassurance than goodbye. But it also leaves me feeling exposed, like she’s seen too much and isn’t judging any of it. Part of me wants to thank her again. Part of me wants to hide. I settle for a quiet nod, hoping that’s enough.


“Well,” she says softly, “I’ll let you both rest.”


She turns and walks toward the back door, her footsteps quiet against the hardwood.


And just like that, she’s gone.


I hold Mia close for a moment longer, breathing her in. Then I set her on the couch with her elephant and grab my laptop from the kitchen counter.


Not until I open it do I see the blinking email alert.


I open my email.


There it is.


Subject: Custody Status Under Review


Content: Upcoming formal home evaluation scheduled.


Required documents: Full guardian history, household schedule, support plans.


My stomach sinks.


This isn’t over.


This is just the beginning.

Chapter 5

The Space Between Us

Sophie

Back in my kitchen, I stare at the half-sliced apple in my hand, then toss it into the bowl with the others. I tell myself I’m just being neighborly. That stepping in during Mia’s school visit wasn’t some huge, defining moment. But my chest is still tight from it.


That feeling, that pull, I thought I’d buried it.


Left it behind with the classroom door I closed in New York.


But it came rushing back.


Sharp. Fast. Familiar.


So strong, it knocked the breath out of me.


I never thought I’d return to Oakridge, jobless, disgraced, and desperate for a fresh start. I used to be someone in New York City. A teacher with purpose. I poured everything into my classroom, every lesson, every laugh, every heartache. Until the day I stood up for a little boy being bullied, a kindergartner who just wanted to feel safe. He was the son of a powerful CEO, and that changed everything.


The media twisted the truth. Painted me as unstable, overly emotional, a threat to my students. None of it was true. But once the headlines stuck, the doors started closing. My contract wasn’t renewed. And no other school wanted someone whose name trended for all the wrong reasons.


I told myself I came back here to regroup. To breathe. But the truth is, I had nowhere else to go. New York spat me out like a bad idea.


My parents had already retired by the coast.


And so, I found myself right back where it all began, at the edge of town, living in my childhood home. Just one white picket fence away from Ethan Ford.


It was the only option left, the last safe place I could land when everything else fell apart.


My childhood home. My memories. My regrets.


The way Mia responded to me. The way Ethan looked at me when I spoke up. Like he was seeing someone he hadn’t expected. Like maybe... I mattered.


I sit at the counter, apple forgotten, and rest my elbows on the cold granite. Who is she really… Mia? She’s sweet, smart, and obviously sensitive. But there’s something else. Like she’s trying so hard to keep everything in order, like she’s watching for something that might break.


Where’s her mom?


I haven’t seen anyone besides Ethan with her. Not once. No pictures. No mentions. It’s just... them. And part of me feels like I’m trespassing into something sacred whenever I ask too much. Still, I can’t help but wonder.


The sharp ring of my phone yanks me from my thoughts. I see the name before I even pick up: Mom.


“Hi, Mom,” I answer, trying to sound upbeat.


“Sophie! How’s everything going, sweetheart?” Her voice is bright, full of sunshine and ocean breeze. They’ve been by the coast for a year now, living out the retirement dream.


“Everything’s fine,” I say, lying a little. “Just settling back in.”


"Have you been eating well?" Mom asks, like clockwork.


"Yes, Mom. I’m not surviving on cereal, I promise."


"You used to live on takeout and coffee," Dad adds. "Don’t pretend we don’t know."


I chuckle. "I’m doing better now. There's even fruit in my fridge. Real fruit. Not the plastic kind."


They laugh together, a familiar, warm sound that almost makes me homesick, though I'm technically home.


"How’s the house?" Dad asks. "Still creaky in all the same spots?"


"Yep. And the upstairs window still whistles when the wind blows. It’s like nothing ever changed."


"Except you," Mom says gently. "You’ve changed, Soph. But that’s not a bad thing."


Her voice falters a little, and for a second, I wonder if they regret not being here with me. But then she adds, "You’ll find your way again. You always do."


Dad’s voice cuts in next. “Anyone bothering you yet? Town still full of nosy neighbors?”


I smile despite myself. “Still nosy. Still neighborly.”


“Don’t let that boy next door give you grief,” Mom adds quickly, not even bothering to mask the concern in her tone.


I flinch. “Which one?”


“You know which one. Ethan Ford.”


I pause. Then softly, “It’s not like that.”


But I wish I knew what it was like. Seeing him with Mia, he looked so... whole. So full of purpose. It makes my stomach twist, and not just from confusion. There’s a pang of jealousy, deep and sharp.


He’s not mine anymore. Maybe he never really was. And now there’s this little girl who calls him Dad, and I don’t even know who she is to him.


"Have you talked to your brother yet?" Dad asks, shifting the tone.


I hesitate. "Not yet. I’ve only been back a few days."


"He mentioned you haven’t stopped by," Mom says gently. "The kids have been asking about Aunt Sophie."


Guilt prickles at my skin. "I will. I just... needed a little time to settle in first."


"He might act tough, but you know Luke," Dad says. "He worries. Especially with you back next to Ethan."


I let out a breath. "Of course he does. That hasn’t changed."


"We’re just saying," Mom adds, "you don’t have to go through everything alone. Even if Oakridge isn’t where you thought you’d land again, you still have people here."


I soften. "Thanks, Mom. I’ll reach out to Luke soon."


"We believe you," she says. "And Sophie?"


"Yeah?"


"Don’t let the past keep you from seeing what’s still possible."


I smile into the phone, the knot in my chest loosening just a little. "I won’t. Love you both."


"We love you too," they say in unison.


I end the call and set the phone down.


For a while, I just sit there, letting the quiet settle around me. Trying to remember how to feel at home in a place I ran from.


A knock at the door breaks the stillness.


I open it to find Ethan on my porch again, looking half-apologetic, half-exhausted. He holds a plastic plate in one hand and runs the other through his hair.


"She’s refusing dinner," he mutters. "Mac and cheese meltdown. I think she’s tired... or overstimulated... or, I don’t know."


I blink, caught off guard. "You want me to…"


"Just come by for a minute? Maybe help with her evening routine? I know it’s asking a lot... but she listens to you."


I hesitate. Luke’s voice flashes in my head: Don’t let him pull you in again. Don’t forget how he left you.


But Mia’s tear-streaked face from earlier pushes back harder. I remember the way her lower lip trembled, how her tiny fists had curled into her lap, her whole body stiff with frustration and overwhelm. There was no tantrum, just quiet frustration, like she was trying not to fall apart in front of anyone.


And something in her eyes, too old for her age, hit me right in the chest. That look of trying to be strong when all you really want is for someone to step in and say, 'It’s okay.'


I nod. “Let me grab my shoes.”


Ethan steps aside as I follow him across the lawn. The moment we step inside, I can feel it, the tension clings to the walls like static.


Miss Dana is crouched beside the dining table, her expression helpless as she tries coaxing Mia to take another bite of dinner.


Mia is in full meltdown mode. Her cheeks are flushed, her tiny fists slam the table, and the sound that comes out of her throat is more frustration than fury, a strangled whimper that tightens my chest.


"She won’t eat," Miss Dana says, eyes wide. "I tried counting down, gave her a couple of options, even grabbed that dinosaur spoon she usually loves, but she’s just not having it tonight."


Ethan lets out a tired breath. “She’s barely said a word all afternoon. Every little thing sets her off.”


Mia pushes the plate away with such force that it clatters, and she bursts into tears.


I drop my bag and kneel beside her without thinking. "Hi, Mia," I say gently, keeping my voice low and calm. "I see you're feeling upset. That's okay. I'm here to help."


Her hands ball tighter in her lap. She doesn’t look at me, but I scoot a little closer.


"Can you tell me with your hands?" I ask, showing her mine.


I tap my stomach. "Is your belly hurting?"


I place my hands like a pillow under my cheek. "Are you sleepy?"


Then I point to the food. "Or maybe you don’t like this today?"


She glances at the plate and shakes her head fast, a tiny whimper escaping. Her breathing is quick, panicky.


Miss Dana hovers in the background, wringing her hands. "She usually likes this. I didn’t know what else to do."


I give her a small nod. "You’re okay. You did your best."


Turning back to Mia, I hold out my fingers. "Let’s do finger talk, okay? One, my tummy hurts. Two, too tired. Three, I don't want the food."


She taps her pointer finger to her palm three times.


"Three? Don’t want the food?"


Mia nods, her lip still trembling.


"Okay, thank you for telling me. No more food right now. We’ll put it away. You are safe."


She sniffles, wipes her cheek, and finally leans slightly toward me.


"Want to sit on the couch with me for a bit? We can pick out stickers soon."


She pauses, then gives me the tiniest nod.


And just like that, the storm begins to pass.


"Too much," she whispers.


I nod. "Okay. No more pushing. Can we clean it up together and make a calm space? Then maybe we can do something fun before bedtime."


She sniffs and nods.


Miss Dana steps back in awe, mouthing, “Thank you.”


Ethan just stares, like he’s seeing Mia, and maybe me, in a whole new light. I see it in the way his shoulders drop, the way his jaw unclenches, even though he doesn’t say a word.


There’s something unspoken in his expression, like he’s not just grateful, but surprised. Surprised I stayed. Surprised I could reach her when he couldn’t. Maybe surprised that the girl he once knew has grown into someone who can handle more than just a broken heart. And in that moment, I wonder if he’s starting to see me as more than just his past.


He stays by the wall at first, arms crossed, jaw tight like he’s holding in more than he knows how to express. I catch him shifting his weight from foot to foot, eyes flicking from Mia to me, like he wants to jump in but doesn’t know how.


Then something softens in him. He moves slowly toward the counter, picks up the plate Mia had pushed away, and quietly clears it without a word. No frustration. No lecture. Just... relief. Like seeing her calm down reminded him that he’s not alone in this.


When I glance up, he meets my eyes with something different than before. Not the usual guarded steel, but something closer to gratitude. Something like hope.


We clean up the food quietly, then I lead Mia into the living room.


“I was just about to help your dad make a bedtime chart,” I say, crouching down. “Want to help us pick out stickers?”


Her small head bobs in a hesitant nod.


That’s all I need. Just one sign that maybe I still know how to do this. How to be useful. How to be me again.


She peeks out from behind the couch, eyes wide but calmer when she sees me.


We settle at the kitchen table, Ethan standing awkwardly to the side as I spread out paper, scissors, glue, and a stack of old magazines I spotted in a bin.


Mia climbs into a chair, still cautious but curious, and starts flipping pages with me. She points at a sun, a toothbrush, a girl brushing her hair.


"Morning," she says softly.


"Perfect," I nod. "Let’s make a chart. We'll split the day into parts: what comes first, what comes next, and what we can look forward to."


Mia starts cutting, and I gently guide her through organizing the pictures into little columns. Ethan leans over now and then, asking questions, his voice softer each time.


“I’ve tried stuff like this,” he murmurs, “but she shuts down fast. I don’t know if I’m doing it right.”


“You’re doing your best,” I reply without looking up. “That counts more than you think.”


His silence tells me he’s not used to hearing that.


As the clock ticks later, I suggest a few learning games she could do over the week. Nothing intense. Just fun. Simple tasks. Predictable structure.


“I can help you plan her days,” I offer quietly while Mia brushes her dolls’ hair. “Nothing rigid. But enough to give her rhythm while school’s out.”


Ethan exhales, like he’s been holding that breath all day. “That would mean a lot.”


We don’t talk much after that. But we don’t need to. It’s in the shared glances. The way he watches Mia’s shoulders drop in relief. The way he lets me take the lead.


He walks me back to my porch hours later.


The air between us is quieter now. More open.


We stop at the steps, neither of us really ready to end the night.


"You’re good with her," he says, his voice softer than usual.


I shrug. "It’s what I used to do. Before everything fell apart."


He hesitates, then adds, "She smiled tonight. For the first time in days. That’s because of you."


I look up at him, the porch light catching the worry lines near his eyes. "It’s not just me. You’re doing more right than you think. You love her in all the ways that matter."


He looks at me for a long moment, like he's trying to figure something out. Or maybe trying to stop himself from saying something he’ll regret.


Finally, he nods. "I’m glad you're here. I mean it."


And this time, I believe him.


-


Later that night, I check my email, expecting nothing but junk.


But a subject line catches my eye:


Open Position — Willow Creek Academy


I click it.


We’ve heard about your work with Mia Ford and your previous teaching background. We’d love to invite you to apply for our early education opening.


My heart jumps. A real chance. A new start.


But as I stare at the screen, the smile fades.


Taking the job might mean stepping away. From Ethan. From Mia. From whatever this is becoming.


And suddenly, I’m not sure what I want more. My career back… or something I never thought I could have.

Chapter 6

Lines That Blur

Ethan

The house feels… different.


I’ve been asking for her more than I want to admit. Not just once or twice when things got rough. Every time Mia reached that point of no return.


The meltdowns, the refusals, the days when I felt like I was hanging by a thread. Sophie was the first name that came to mind.


And the wild part? She always showed up. No hesitation. No judgment. Just showed up.


Sometimes she even comes over without being asked, like she just knows. She’ll knock gently, peek in, and say something like, "Thought I’d check in on my favorite girl."


She means Mia. But I’d be lying if I said part of me doesn’t feel seen in that moment too.


I never thought I’d rely on Sophie Taylor. Not like this. Not after all those years of distance and silence.


But here we are.


I didn’t plan to ask for her help so often. I’d meant to handle everything on my own. I always do.


But the words had slipped out, and instead of pulling away, she stepped in without hesitation.


And now, she’s in our world more than I ever imagined. Not because I asked again, but because she kept showing up. Softly. Steadily.


It’s not loud. Not exactly silent, either. It’s alive in a way that feels steady and warm, like the space finally knows how to breathe again.


There’s still the sound of Mia’s tiny footsteps sprinting down the hall, the occasional crash of a dropped spoon, and the daily soundtrack of cereal crunching beneath someone’s sock.


But now there’s something else under it all.


A hum.


It’s Sophie. Not in any obvious way. She doesn’t fill the room with chatter or rearrange the furniture. But somehow, her presence has become part of the house, like the scent of cinnamon after baking or the faint echo of music that lingers long after it stops playing.


I start noticing it in strange moments.


Like yesterday, when the fire alarm wouldn’t stop beeping. The batteries had died, and of course, I didn’t have any spares. Mia was already on edge after therapy, clinging to her elephant and covering her ears. I tried everything—resetting it, pulling it down, even muttering a few words I wouldn’t want her repeating.


Then there was a knock at the door.


Sophie stood there, holding a pack of batteries. "Heard the chirping through the wall. Thought I’d save you from losing your mind."


I didn’t argue. I just stepped aside, and she came in like she belonged here. Mia calmed the moment Sophie crouched down and handed her a plush toy from her bag. Then she handed me a screwdriver like she’d done this a dozen times before.


That was the moment I stopped pretending she was just a helpful neighbor. She knew exactly how to step in without making me feel smaller.


Like this morning.


I’m standing in front of the pantry, refilling Mia’s snack drawer before she wakes up. My mind’s half on work emails, half on her lunchbox. I pop open a box of fruit cups and start stacking them—bottoms up, because they fit better that way.


“There’s a method to your madness, huh?”


I jump. She’s behind me, hair pulled into a messy braid, wearing one of those soft sweaters that make it hard not to stare too long.


“You organizing snacks or hiding them for later?”


“They’re still reachable,” I say, straightening.


She walks over, plucks one fruit cup from the top, and flips it. “This isn’t a corporate strategy, Ethan. It’s applesauce.”


I chuckle, reaching for a box of granola bars. “Efficiency is a virtue.”


“You’re lucky she doesn’t mind upside-down peaches.”


Her grin is teasing, and it stays in my head long after she walks back toward the living room.


Later that day, I come downstairs after taking a call and stop in my tracks. Mia’s giggling echoes off the walls.


The coffee table is gone. The couch cushions are scattered. A massive pillow fort made of blankets, chairs, and even a mop for a flagpole sprawls across the floor.


Sophie’s crawling out from one of the tunnels, wearing Margot’s old apron, now covered in crayon streaks and marker fingerprints. Her hair’s coming loose from its braid, and she’s laughing like a kid herself.


Mia peeks out from behind a blanket, face flushed, curls a mess, eyes bright.


I lean against the doorframe.


Sophie notices. “You judging my engineering skills, Mr. CEO?”


I shake my head. “Not judging. Just wondering how you got her to smile that wide.”


Her eyes soften. “She just needed a mission. Today’s mission was to build the comfiest fortress in Oakridge.”


I swallow. “Looks like a five-star operation.”


Mia beams and dives back into the blanket cave.


After dinner, with Margot long gone and Mia winding down, Sophie and I sit at the kitchen table working on her behavior chart. The table is scattered with stickers: stars, smiley faces, cartoon animals. Mia’s asleep upstairs. For now.


We lean closer over the paper, pointing to morning tasks: “Brush teeth,” “Eat breakfast,” “Get dressed.”


I reach for the sheet of sun stickers just as Sophie does.


Our hands brush.


She freezes.


So do I.


Just for a second.


She pulls back first, fingers twitching slightly. Her eyes dart down to the paper. “You’re smudging the smiley faces.”


I clear my throat. “Didn’t mean to invade your creative process.”


“I take sticker placement very seriously,” she mutters.


Her cheeks are a little pink. Mine probably are, too.


We fall into silence again. But it’s not uncomfortable. Just charged in a way I don’t fully understand.


We talk softly about Mia’s progress. About her new favorite word: “bubble.” About the moment she tried to teach Sophie how to dance like her dolls. And how Sophie actually tried.


I laughed. Out loud. I can’t remember the last time I did that.


Then Sophie shared a story about one of her old students in New York who once made a puppet show about dinosaurs who went to outer space because they were “bored of volcanoes.”


And I listened. Not just nodded, but actually listened. Noticing the way her voice got soft in the middle of a memory, or how her eyes lit up when she talked about kids who felt misunderstood.


“You miss it, don’t you?” I ask quietly.


She doesn’t answer right away. Then, softly, “Every day.”


I hesitate. “You were good at it.”


She glances up, surprised. “How would you know?”


I shrug. “I just watched you bring Mia back from a full-blown meltdown using sticker charts and dinosaur puppets.”


Her smile is slow. “Those were unicorns.”


“Right. My bad. Very ferocious unicorns.”


She laughs again, brushing a curl behind her ear. Her eyes shimmer a little, voice quieter now. “Thanks. That means more than you know.”


She looks down at the chart, then back at me. “You know, this... this isn’t just helping Mia.”


“What do you mean?”


“It’s helping me too,” she says. “Being here. Doing something that matters. It makes me feel like I still have a place.”


I sit back, watching her. “You do.”


She bites her lip, and for a second, there’s something raw and vulnerable in her eyes. “I’m afraid of messing it up again. Of getting attached and then watching it fall apart.”


I nod slowly. “Yeah. I know that feeling.”


She tilts her head. “Do you?”


I pause. “You ever try to hold everything together for someone who’s already gone?”


She blinks. “Ethan...”


I stand and walk to the sink, needing a second to steady myself. “Mia’s not my daughter. She’s my niece. Her parents, my younger brother Andrew and his wife Rachel, were killed in a car accident two years ago. Just like that, I became her guardian.”


I grip the edge of the sink, forcing the rest out. “Rachel was warm, kind, patient. Andrew was the funny one, always got Mia to laugh with the silliest voices. They were good people. Great parents. And then one rainy night… they were gone. And I had this little girl with wide eyes and no words, just grief she didn’t know how to name.”
