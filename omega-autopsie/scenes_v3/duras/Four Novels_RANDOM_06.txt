He waits for the passing of his intense surprise at discovering that he is not dying from believing so strongly that he is dying. Filled with this impossible desire to change his feelings, to love differently, he looks at the trees with all his strength, begging himself to find them beautiful. But they are of no help to him. He imagines that other child, so delightful, who is watching, without being able to see it at the edge of the pond, the imperceptible growth of the grass forcing its way toward daylight, but she is of no help to him. His preference for Valérie, his child, still remains shining and indescribable. That was it. “That man, how rude he is,” he continues. In vain. Oh, how he tries to get back into this long wait to which he has relegated himself long before and which he can so conveniently call his despair! Oh! let Valérie’s blondness roam the world, let the whole world look dull, if it so desires, next to so much blondness, why should one think about that? Mr. Andesmas thinks. At the same time knowing that one cannot think about it. And, if it were thinkable, why think about it with this crushing pain and not tenderly? Mr. Andesmas goes on thinking, knowing that he is lying, that one can only attempt to think about it with terrible pain. This young, hatefully young pain lasted, Mr. Andesmas claimed. How long?
