Sagra, it was obvious, had not been a city at all, little more than a sort of trading post, a tiny checkerboard of intersecting streets beside the lagoon. The ground floors revealing fragments of vaulted rooms in the rear, the spacious cellars over which the paving was falling in along the gutters, betokened warehouses and shops; a few ghosts of handsome villas appeared, crouching in their teeming gardens as though behind a fortress of underbrush. But the motionless gloom caught up these ordinary fragments into a magic crystal, and a reverie was spun by the murmur of fountains that seemed still to recall the vanished occupants to their humble tasks, to braid into a mute garland round well and pond those profound gestures which make the heart leap like a panic sentiment of life’s permanence. I was suddenly filled with an anxious desire to awaken, if only for a moment, the echoes in these streets, to shout for some living soul forgotten in this labyrinth of silence. But of course there was no one. The daylight was beginning to wane in these somber alleys, and I was just deciding to leave when I thought I made out a faint sound of waves, and almost immediately emerged, to my surprise, on the ridge above an irregular basin of lapping water which had once—how long ago?—been the harbor of Sagra. Huge trees encircled it, trailing their lowest branches in the water, leaving a brighter patch only in the middle.
