The last wooded slopes of the Tängri, descending to the sea in gentle lines, formed the painting’s background. The bird’s-eye view, naively steep, truncated the mountain’s peak, whose converging lines from the lower foothills nonetheless suggested the imminent and vital enormity of the mass, as if the grasp of a giant paw had gripped the landscape from the edge of the frame down to the sea. At the water’s edge, the sparkling afternoon sunlight played over the amphitheater of houses and the town ramparts like a sea-borne mirage. Rhages appeared, caught in the loving torpor of its siesta, with all the indolent activity of its terraces, the mild somnambulism of the tiny figures which ventured into its streets. A rich pelt of flames, forming architectural scrolls, constituted a border for the besieged town. The disturbing impression produced by this vision of a massacre derived from the extraordinarily natural and even relaxed character Longhone’s serene cruelty had succeeded in imparting to his painting. Rhages was burning the way a flower opens, without laceration and without drama: rather than a conflagration, it seemed the peaceful unfurling, the tranquil voracity of a greedier vegetation, a burning bush outlining and crowning the town, the opening scroll of a rose around the swarm of insects at its heart. Orsenna’s fleet was ranged in a semicircle in the town’s harbor, but the wall of calm smoke rising in heavy puffs from the sea suggested not so much the deafening fracas of artillery as some picturesque and visitable cataclysm, the volcano of the Tängri once again dousing its lava flows in the sea.
