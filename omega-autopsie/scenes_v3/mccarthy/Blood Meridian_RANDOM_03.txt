The Christ jostled past, a poor ɹgure of straw with carven head and feet. He wore a crown of mountain briars and on his brow were painted drops of blood and on his old dry wooden cheeks blue tears. The villagers knelt and blessed themselves and some stepped forward and touched the garment the ɹgure wore and kissed their ɹngertips. The parade trundled past mournfully and small children sat in the doorways eating pastry skulls and watching the parade and the rain in the streets. The judge sat alone in the cantina. He also watched the rain, his eyes small in his great naked face. He’d ɹlled his pockets with little candy deathsheads and he sat by the door and oʃered these to children passing on the walk under the eaves but they shied away like little horses. In the evening groups of townfolk descended from the cemetery on the side of the hill and later in the dark by candle or lamp light they emerged again and made their way up to the church to pray. None but passed clutches of Americans crazed with drink and these grimy visitants would doʃ their hats oaɹshly and totter and grin and make obscene suggestions to the young girls. Carroll had closed his squalid bistro at dusk but opened it again to save the doors being stove. Sometime in the night a party of horsemen bound for California arrived, every man of them slumped in exhaustion. Yet within the hour they’d ridden out again.
