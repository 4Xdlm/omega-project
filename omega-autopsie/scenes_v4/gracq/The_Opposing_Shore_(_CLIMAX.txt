The usher who had taken charge of me upon my appearance at the palace doors (one never walked unaccompanied through the Council’s corridors) showed me into a dim and rather low-ceilinged hall on an upper floor. A long, heavy table filled one corner; strangely massive and set off with carved plaques, it oddly evoked, in this crumbling palace, the barbarous ages of Orsenna—iron crowns set with rough stones, all the splendid and savage ceremonial of the Lombard ages. The walls, as everywhere in the palace, were covered from floor to ceiling with dark leather; the windows, with their narrow leading and translucent panes, admitted no more than a diffused and exhausted glow, as if the palace had sunk to the bottom of a sealed courtyard; one of these windows, however, was open, revealing a narrow rectangle where the clear sky was already darkening to night, and the eye—attracted as though it were a prisoner’s—plunged down the city’s slope toward the horizon of distant woods; gradually the gleam of a lamp set on a bracket near the huge table extinguished the vestiges of daylight and gave the room a look of anonymous intimacy, the sort of calm vigilance one might expect in a chapel, inspiring in me the secure and infinitely comforting sense that I was expected.
