The platform stretched before her like a concrete prayer, empty save for the weight of her solitude. Overhead lights flickered awake in the gathering gloom, casting amber pools that barely touched the edges of her worn leather boots. She pulled her coat tighter against the October chill, watching her breath bloom and dissolve in the crystalline air.

The rails gleamed like silver veins beneath the settling mist, twin arteries that pulsed with the promise of elsewhere. They vanished into the gray throat of evening, swallowed by fog that rolled in from the distant hills like spilled milk across dark cloth. How strange that metal could look so liquid, so alive with possibility and finality intertwined.

Behind her lay the town that had cradled her for thirty-seven years—every brick building a chapter, every cracked sidewalk a verse in the poem of her existence. The bakery where she'd bought Sunday croissants still warm from ovens that had fed three generations. Mrs. Chen's garden where hollyhocks stood sentinel against white picket fences, their faces turned toward light that no longer came. The library steps where she'd first kissed David beneath a canopy of autumn maples, their leaves falling like confetti for a wedding that would never be.

A distant whistle threaded through the mist. Coming.

She thought of her mother's china, wrapped now in newspaper and nestled in cardboard tombs. Each piece held conversations—Christmas mornings when steam rose from coffee cups like incense, birthday celebrations where cake crumbs gathered in saucers painted with forget-me-nots. The weight of abandoning objects that had witnessed love seemed heavier than the suitcase at her feet.

The approaching headlight carved through fog like a lighthouse beam seeking shipwrecked souls. She pressed her palm against the ticket in her pocket, the paper soft from repeated folding and unfolding, each crease a moment of doubt conquered. Portland. A city she knew only through photographs and promises of employment, where rain fell differently and strangers walked streets that held no memory of her footsteps.

What did courage feel like? Not the fierce roar she'd expected, but something quieter—a steady hum beneath her ribs, a tuning fork struck against the bones of necessity. The train's rumble grew stronger, vibrations climbing through the platform into her legs, her spine, until her entire body thrummed with the frequency of departure.

The locomotive emerged from the mist like some ancient creature returning from exile, its windows glowing yellow against the deepening dusk. She lifted her suitcase, surprised by how light it felt now, as if the approaching train had already begun to pull gravity away from familiar things.

The brakes sang their metallic lullaby. Doors would open soon, revealing the warm interior where strangers sat with their own stories of leaving, their own catalogs of what remained behind. She straightened her shoulders, tasting the sharp sweetness of endings on her tongue.

The train exhaled steam and possibility. She stepped forward into the space between who she had been and who she might become.
