The dresser holds its breath. Mahogany lungs, polished decades ago by Eleanor's careful hands, now exhale dust motes that spiral through amber afternoon light. Harold's fingers trace the wood grain—rivers flowing toward an ocean that swallowed his boy three months past.

A baseball glove sprawls across the unmade bed. Leather tongue lolling, waiting for the catch that will never come. The mitt remembers Tommy's palm, the salt-sweet constellation of a thirteen-year-old's grip. Harold lifts it, inhales the ghost of summer grass and Cracker Jack prizes.

"Strike three!" The umpire's voice echoes from 1987. Tommy's shoulders sagging in defeat, then brightening as Harold ruffled his hair. "Next season, champ." But next season became next decade, became Tommy in Afghanistan, became a folded flag and Harold alone with objects that refuse to forget.

The window frames October's dying. Maple leaves surrender their chlorophyll dreams, bleeding crimson against glass that Tommy once fogged with his breath, drawing stick figures in the condensation. Harold watches shadows lengthen across Spider-Man wallpaper—web-slinger frozen mid-swing, cape billowing toward adventures that exist only in four-color panels now.

A music box sits sentinel on the nightstand. Ballerina trapped in her eternal pirouette, pink tutu faded to the color of old roses. Tommy's secret treasure, inherited from his grandmother. Harold winds the key. Für Elise stumbles through mechanical measures while the dancer spins her lonely ballet.

The melody summons Eleanor's voice: "He's sensitive, that one. Don't you dare tease him." But Harold never did. Instead, he sat cross-legged on this same carpet, watching his son choreograph elaborate stories where the ballerina rescued kingdoms and slayed dragons with nothing but grace and determination.

Dust particles perform their own dance in the slanted light. Each speck a memory suspended: Tommy's first steps across these floorboards. His fevered breathing during chicken pox. The rustle of comic book pages at midnight when he thought his parents couldn't hear.

The closet door hangs ajar. Inside, clothes wait like empty promises. School uniforms pressed and patient. A Halloween costume—Batman cape draped over miniature shoulders that grew broad enough to carry a rifle, strong enough to serve his country, fragile enough to stop a roadside bomb outside Kandahar.

Harold's reflection fragments in the dresser mirror. Old man, young father, every age between. Time collapses like a house of cards. He is thirty-five again, teaching Tommy to tie his shoes. He is fifty, proud at graduation. He is seventy-three, burying dreams in Arlington soil.

The baseball glove slips from his fingers. It lands softly, a whispered promise on carpet worn thin by decades of footsteps—small ones racing toward breakfast, heavy ones pacing during teenage heartbreak, combat boots during that last leave.

Darkness creeps across the room. Objects retreat into shadow, but their voices linger. The dresser sighs. The music box falls silent. The mirror holds its fractured light.

Harold remains, surrounded by the archaeology of love.
