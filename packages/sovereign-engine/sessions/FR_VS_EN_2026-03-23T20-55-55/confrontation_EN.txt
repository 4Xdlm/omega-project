The Crossroads Tavern squatted against the desert like a scab on burnt skin, its neon sign flickering epileptic red through the dust-choked twilight while eighteen-wheelers thundered past on Highway 89, their diesel exhaust mixing with the perpetual smell of stale beer and decades-old cigarette smoke that had seeped so deeply into the wood paneling it had become part of the building's molecular structure.

Marcus pushed through the door. The hinges screamed.

Inside, fluorescent tubes buzzed overhead like dying insects, casting everything in that particular shade of institutional yellow that made healthy skin look jaundiced and already-sick skin look corpse-pale, while the jukebox in the corner sat silent and dark, its chrome surfaces reflecting the sparse interior in funhouse distortions.

Three customers. Maybe four if you counted the shape slumped in the back booth, though from the absolute stillness of the figure it might have been a pile of old clothes or a man who'd given up breathing sometime around noon.

The bartender was a woman in her sixties with silver hair pulled back in a bun so tight it seemed to stretch the wrinkles from her forehead, and when she looked up from the glass she was polishing—the same glass she'd been working on for the past ten minutes, polishing it with the mechanical persistence of someone who'd learned long ago that idle hands led to idle thoughts and idle thoughts led to places she didn't want to go—her eyes held that particular wariness that came from serving drinks to truckers and drifters and people running from things they couldn't outrun.

"What'll it be?" Her voice carried the rasp of a lifetime smoker.

Marcus settled onto a barstool. The vinyl cracked under his weight. "Whiskey. Whatever you've got that won't blind me."

She poured two fingers of something amber into a glass that had seen better decades, and when she slid it across the bar the liquid caught the fluorescent light and threw it back in amber fractals that reminded Marcus, for just a moment, of sunlight through his sister's hair on that last morning when she'd stood in the kitchen doorway, twenty-two years old and beautiful and stupid with trust, telling him she was driving out to meet Danny because Danny had finally decided to leave his wife, because Danny had promised they'd go to California together, because Danny loved her the way she'd always dreamed of being loved.

The whiskey burned. Good.

"Expecting someone?" the bartender asked, because Marcus kept checking the door and she'd been in this business long enough to recognize the signs—the coiled tension in his shoulders, the way his fingers drummed against the bar, the fact that he'd positioned himself with clear sightlines to both the entrance and the back exit.

"Maybe." Marcus lifted the glass again. "You know a man named Danny Kowalski?"

Something shifted in her expression. Subtle. Like a curtain being drawn.

"Might do. Might not." She went back to polishing glasses. "Depends who's asking."

The door opened again and a trucker wandered in, all belly and beard and the particular swagger of a man who'd been driving for sixteen hours straight and needed to prove to himself he could still walk in a straight line. He ordered a beer, complained about the heat, left a dollar tip, and disappeared into the men's room, where the sound of his pissing echoed through the thin walls like rain on a tin roof.

Marcus waited. The clock above the bar ticked toward eight-thirty.

He'd been waiting ten years. Another few minutes wouldn't kill him.

Though someone might die tonight.

The thought came unbidden, and with it the memory of standing in the county morgue while a bored technician pulled back the sheet to reveal what the desert had made of his sister after three weeks under the sun—skin like leather, eye sockets empty, her beautiful hair reduced to brittle strands that crumbled at a touch. The coroner had ruled it suicide. Case closed. No investigation needed for another junkie who'd wandered into the wasteland to shoot up and never wandered back out.

But Marcus knew better. Had always known.

Because Sarah had been clean for eight months when she died, and because the needle they'd found beside her body had been inserted into her left arm even though Sarah was right-handed, and because the last person to see her alive had been Danny Kowalski, married father of two, who'd sworn to the sheriff that he'd never laid eyes on Sarah Chen in his life.

The bartender was watching him in the mirror behind the bottles. "You're not from around here."

"Phoenix." Marcus finished his whiskey. "But I grew up in Flagstaff. Long time ago."

"Flagstaff's nice. Cool summers."

"Yeah. It was."

The trucker emerged from the bathroom, bought a bag of peanuts, and left. The door swung shut behind him with a sound like a coffin lid closing. Now it was just Marcus, the bartender, and whoever was decomposing in the back booth.

Eight forty-five.

Marcus had done his research. Ten years of careful investigation, following paper trails and asking questions and slowly, methodically building a case that would never see the inside of a courtroom because the statute of limitations had run out and because proving murder when the body had been reduced to jerky and bones was next to impossible.

But he'd found the witnesses who remembered seeing Danny's truck parked behind the Motel 6 on the night Sarah disappeared. Had tracked down the desk clerk who remembered Danny paying cash for a room, remembered him asking specifically for one on the back side of the building where the parking lot wasn't visible from the street.

Had located the dealer who'd sold Danny the heroin.

"Your friend Danny," Marcus said to the bartender, because he could see in her eyes that she knew exactly who Danny Kowalski was. "He still come around here much?"

She set down the glass she'd been polishing and picked up another one. "Like I said. Depends who's asking."

"Someone who knew his girlfriend. Sarah Chen. Pretty girl. Asian. Had a butterfly tattoo on her shoulder."

The bartender's hands went still. For just a moment. Then she resumed polishing.

"Don't know any Sarah Chen."

But her voice had changed. Gotten smaller.

Marcus pulled out his wallet and extracted a photograph—Sarah at twenty-one, laughing at something off-camera, her hair catching the light like spun gold, that butterfly tattoo visible on her bare shoulder. He placed it on the bar between them.

The bartender looked at it. Looked away.

"Pretty girl," Marcus said. "She'd be thirty-two now. If she'd lived."

"I told you. I don't—"

"She died out in the desert ten years ago. Needle in her arm. Cops said suicide."

The bartender's knuckles had gone white around the glass. "Maybe she was just... maybe it was an accident. People make mistakes."

"Sarah didn't use. Hadn't touched the stuff in months." Marcus leaned forward slightly, his voice dropping to just above a whisper. "But someone wanted it to look like she did."

The door opened.

Marcus felt it before he saw it—a change in the air pressure, a shift in the quality of light, the way prey animals must feel when the wolf finally steps into the clearing. He didn't turn around. Not yet. Instead he watched the mirror behind the bar as a man entered, silhouetted against the dying light outside.

Danny Kowalski had aged badly. The lean frame Marcus remembered from high school had gone soft around the middle, and his hair—once thick and dark and perpetually falling into his eyes in that way that had made half the girls in school go weak in the knees—had retreated to a thin fringe around his skull. His face carried the particular puffiness of a man who'd spent too many years drowning his problems in alcohol.

But his eyes. Christ. His eyes were exactly the same.

Danny approached the bar slowly, and Marcus could smell him now—old sweat and cheaper cologne and something else, something sour and desperate that might have been fear.

"Evening, Dolores." Danny's voice had roughened over the years, taken on the gravelly quality of a longtime smoker. "The usual."

The bartender—Dolores—poured whiskey into a fresh glass with hands that trembled just slightly. Danny took the drink and settled onto a stool three seats down from Marcus, close enough to talk but far enough away to run if running became necessary.

They sat in silence for a long moment, two men drinking whiskey in a roadside bar while the desert wind howled outside and the fluorescent lights buzzed overhead like angry wasps trapped in amber.

Danny knew. Marcus could see it in the set of his shoulders, the way he kept glancing toward the door, the fact that he'd chosen a seat with his back to the wall. The guilty always knew.

"Hot today," Danny said finally.

"Yeah." Marcus lifted his glass. "Supposed to cool off tonight."

"That's what they say." Danny took a sip of whiskey. "You passing through?"

"Something like that."

More silence. Dolores had retreated to the far end of the bar and was pretending to organize bottles, but Marcus could feel her listening, waiting for something to explode.

"You look familiar," Danny said. "We meet before?"

Marcus turned on his stool to face him fully. "Marcus Chen. We went to high school together. Flagstaff High. Class of '98."

Danny's face went through a series of micro-expressions—recognition, calculation, fear, and finally a kind of resigned understanding that told Marcus everything he needed to know.

"Marcus Chen." Danny repeated the name slowly, tasting it. "Sarah's brother."

"That's right."

"I heard... I heard about Sarah. Real sorry for your loss."

The words hung in the air between them like smoke. Marcus set down his glass with deliberate care.

"Are you."

"Course I am. She was... she was a sweet girl."

"Sweet enough to fuck behind your wife's back for six months."

Danny's hand tightened around his glass. "I don't know what you think you know, but—"

"I know you were screwing my sister while telling her you were going to leave your wife. I know you promised her California. I know she believed you because she was twenty-two and stupid and thought love meant something." Marcus's voice remained level, conversational, but something cold had crept into his eyes. "I know she drove out to meet you on the night of August fifteenth, ten years ago, because she thought you were finally going to make good on all those promises."

Danny was sweating now despite the air conditioning. "Look, Marcus—"

"I know she died that night with a needle in her arm even though she'd been clean for eight months. I know the heroin came from Jimmy Torrino, and I know you bought it from him three days before Sarah died because Jimmy remembers you, Danny. Remembers you asking specifically for the pure stuff. The kind that would kill someone who wasn't used to it."

"You're crazy." But Danny's voice lacked conviction. "Sarah was a junkie. Everyone knew that."

"Sarah was getting better. Going to meetings. Had a job lined up in Tucson." Marcus leaned forward slightly. "But then she found out about the baby."

Danny went very still.

"Yeah. I know about that too. Took me a while to piece it together, but I got there eventually. Sarah was pregnant. With your kid. And suddenly all those promises about leaving your wife didn't seem so appealing anymore, did they?"

"She never told me—"

"She never got the chance." Marcus's voice had dropped to barely above a whisper. "Because when she showed up at that motel room thinking you were going to drive off into the sunset together, you had other plans."

Danny stood up abruptly, knocking over his stool. "I don't have to listen to this."

"Sit down."

Something in Marcus's tone made Danny freeze halfway to the door. When he turned back, Marcus was still sitting at the bar, still holding his whiskey, but there was something different about him now. Something predatory.

"I said sit down."

Danny looked toward Dolores, but the bartender had disappeared into the back room. The figure in the corner booth hadn't moved. They were alone.

Slowly, Danny righted his stool and sat back down.

"That's better." Marcus took another sip of whiskey. "Now. Where were we? Oh right. The motel room. You want to tell me what happened, or should I fill in the blanks myself?"

"Nothing happened. I never—"

"You held her down." Marcus's voice was perfectly calm, but his knuckles had gone white around the glass. "She was small. Hundred and ten pounds soaking wet. Easy to overpower. You held her down and you injected her with enough heroin to kill a horse, and then you waited for her to die."

"That's not—she wanted it. She begged me for it."

The words slipped out before Danny could stop them. Marcus went very still.

"She begged you."

"She was... she was hysterical. Crying about the baby, about how we were going to be together. I tried to explain that it wasn't that simple, that I had responsibilities, but she wouldn't listen." Danny's voice was gaining speed now, the words tumbling out like water through a broken dam. "She said she'd tell my wife. Said she'd ruin me. I just wanted her to calm down, to think clearly."

"So you gave her heroin."

"Just a little. Just enough to... but she grabbed the needle. Said if I wouldn't leave with her, she'd rather be dead." Danny's face was flushed now, sweat beading on his forehead. "It was suicide, Marcus. Just like the cops said."

Marcus set down his glass and turned to face Danny fully. "You're lying."

"I'm not—"

"You're lying because Sarah was afraid of needles. Had been since we were kids. Wouldn't even get a flu shot without someone holding her hand." Marcus's voice was getting quieter, more controlled. "You're lying because the injection site was on her left arm and Sarah was right-handed. You're lying because you dumped her body twenty miles from the motel and drove back home to your wife and kids like nothing happened."

Danny was shaking now. "You can't prove any of that."

"No. I can't." Marcus stood up slowly. "Statute of limitations ran out years ago. Even if I could prove you murdered her, which I can't, you'd walk free."

"Then why—"

"Because some things don't have statutes of limitations."

Marcus reached into his jacket and Danny flinched, but instead of a weapon Marcus pulled out a small digital recorder. He set it on the bar between them.

"Everything you just said. About holding her down. About the needle. About dumping her body." Marcus smiled, and it was not a pleasant expression. "Your wife might be interested to hear it. Your kids too, when they're old enough. Your employer. The local newspaper."

Danny stared at the recorder like it was a coiled rattlesnake.

"Course, there might be another way to handle this."

"What do you want?"

Marcus reached into his other pocket and pulled out a small plastic bag containing white powder. He placed it next to the recorder.

"Same stuff you gave Sarah. Pure as the driven snow. Enough to kill a man your size in about three minutes."

Danny's eyes went wide. "You're insane."

"Maybe. Ten years of thinking about this will do that to a person." Marcus picked up his whiskey glass and drained it. "Here's how this works. You can take what's in that bag, or I can play what's on this recorder for everyone you've ever met. Your choice."

"I won't do it."

"Then you'll live with the consequences." Marcus pocketed the recorder. "Your wife will leave you. Take the kids. Your boss will fire you. Everyone in this shithole town will know what you are."

Danny was hyperventilating now. "Please. I have children."

"So did Sarah. Would have, anyway."

"It was an accident. I swear to God it was an accident."

"No. It wasn't." Marcus leaned closer. "You planned it. Bought the heroin three days in advance. Picked a motel with no security cameras. Drove her body out to the desert and left her for the coyotes."

"She was going to ruin everything."

"So you killed her."

The words hung in the air like a death sentence. Danny stared at the bag of heroin, then at Marcus, then back at the bag.

"How do I know you won't release the recording anyway?"

"You don't."

"That's not much of a guarantee."

"It's the only one you're getting."

Danny picked up the bag with trembling fingers. The powder inside shifted like snow in a snow globe.

"I loved her," he said quietly. "I really did."

"No. You didn't." Marcus stood up and threw a twenty on the bar. "If you'd loved her, she'd still be alive."

He walked toward the door, then stopped and looked back.

"You've got five minutes before I call the sheriff. If you're going to do it, do it now."

Marcus pushed through the door and stepped out into the desert night. The air was cooler now, filled with the smell of sage and distant rain. He walked to his truck and sat behind the wheel, engine off, windows down.

Waiting.

Through the bar's grimy window he could see Danny still sitting at the bar, staring at the bag in his hands. The man looked small under the fluorescent lights. Broken.

Good.

Marcus started the engine and pulled out of the parking lot. In his rearview mirror he could see the Crossroads Tavern growing smaller, its neon sign flickering red against the darkness.

He didn't look back again.
