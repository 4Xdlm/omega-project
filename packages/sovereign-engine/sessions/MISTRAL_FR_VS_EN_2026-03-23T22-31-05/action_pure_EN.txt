**The Night is a Labyrinth with Teeth**

The first gunshot was a misfire of silence—no crack, no echo, just the sudden *absence* of sound where there should have been one, like a held breath before a scream. Then the second came, a wet *thwack* against stucco, and the pigeons erupted from the rooftops in a flurry of panicked wings, their shadows stitching the moonlit sky into jagged fragments. Elias didn’t look back. He *knew* the sound of a suppressed pistol, knew the way it turned the air to syrup, knew the man behind it would not miss a third time.

The alley swallowed him whole.

Cobblestones slick with the day’s heat, now cooling into something treacherous, slick as a butcher’s floor. His soles found purchase in the grooves between stones, the rhythm of his steps a stuttering heartbeat against the city’s pulse. Naples at night was a living thing, a beast that breathed in the scent of salt and diesel, exhaled in the clatter of shutters and the distant wail of a scooter’s engine. It did not care for the chase. It only *endured*.

Elias’s lungs burned. Not from exertion—he was lean, wiry, built for this—but from the knowledge that every breath was a gamble. The man behind him, *Vasquez*, was not a hunter. He was a *reaper*. And reapers did not tire.

A left turn, sharp as a blade. The alley narrowed, walls pressing in like the ribs of some great, slumbering animal. Laundry lines sagged overhead, ghostly sheets flapping in the sea breeze, their edges brushing his shoulders like the fingers of the dead. He ducked beneath a dangling bulb, its filament buzzing like a trapped insect, and the light painted his shadow long and skeletal against the wall. For a heartbeat, he was not Elias the thief, Elias the survivor—he was just a man, a flicker of motion in the dark, insignificant as a moth.

Then the alley spat him out into a piazza, and the city roared back to life.

The square was a stage set for a play no one had rehearsed. A fountain at its center, Neptune’s trident raised to the sky, water arcing in silver parabolas that caught the moonlight and shattered it into a thousand shards. Tourists—foolish, oblivious—laughed over gelato, their voices thin and brittle as spun sugar. A street musician sawed at a violin, the notes sour and discordant, as if the instrument itself were weeping. Elias wove between them, a shadow slipping through the cracks of their reality.

Behind him, Vasquez emerged like a shark breaking the surface.

No shout. No warning. The reaper moved with the quiet precision of a man who had long since accepted that the world was a place of prey and predators, and he had chosen his role. His suit was immaculate, charcoal gray, the fabric drinking the light rather than reflecting it. His tie was knotted with surgical precision, the knot a perfect Windsor, as if he had dressed for a funeral—*his own*, perhaps, or Elias’s.

Elias risked a glance. Vasquez’s face was a mask of calm, his dark eyes flat and unblinking, like a doll’s. Only his hands betrayed him—long-fingered, elegant, the left one flexing as if already wrapped around Elias’s throat.

*Think. Don’t just run.*

The fountain. The water. The tourists.

Elias veered left, skirting the edge of the piazza, his path a serpentine coil. The violinist’s bow screeched, the note hanging in the air like a curse. A woman in a sundress turned, her mouth open in surprise, gelato dripping from her cone onto the cobblestones in a pink Rorschach blot. Elias’s shoulder clipped hers, sending her stumbling, and he hissed an apology that was swallowed by the night.

Vasquez was gaining.

The reaper moved like liquid, his steps silent, his breathing even. He did not run. He *flowed*. And in that flow, Elias saw the truth: Vasquez was not chasing him. He was *herding* him.

Toward what?

The answer came in the form of a scooter, its engine a snarling beast, parked at the mouth of a side street. The rider—a kid, no older than twenty, his face obscured by a helmet—revved the throttle, the sound a challenge. Elias didn’t hesitate. He lunged, grabbing the handlebars, wrenching the bike from the kid’s grip. The rider yelped, tumbling into the street as Elias swung a leg over the seat.

“Sorry!” he shouted, though he wasn’t, not really.

The scooter bucked beneath him, the engine coughing like a smoker’s first drag. He twisted the throttle, and the bike lurched forward, tires spitting gravel. The piazza blurred into streaks of light and shadow, the tourists mere smears of color. Behind him, Vasquez did not slow. He *walked*, his steps unhurried, his gaze locked onto Elias like a missile’s guidance system.

Then the scooter’s engine died.

A sputter. A cough. A final, wheezing gasp. Elias cursed, kicking at the starter, but the machine was dead, a mechanical corpse. He abandoned it, leaping onto the cobblestones, his knees absorbing the impact with a jolt that rattled his teeth. The side street ahead was a tunnel of darkness, the buildings leaning in like conspirators. Somewhere, a dog barked, the sound sharp and accusatory.

He ran.

The street twisted, a serpent’s spine, and Elias followed its curves, his breath ragged, his mind a whirlwind of calculations. Left. Right. Left again. The city was a maze, but every maze had a pattern, a logic. He just had to find it before Vasquez did.

Ahead, a staircase, its steps worn smooth by centuries of feet. He took them two at a time, his thighs burning, his lungs screaming. At the top, a terrace, its edge lined with potted lemon trees, their leaves rustling like whispered secrets. Below, the city sprawled, a glittering cancer of light and shadow. The Bay of Naples glowed in the distance, the water a sheet of hammered silver, the islands floating like mirages.

And there, at the bottom of the stairs, Vasquez.

The reaper paused, his head tilting slightly, as if listening to a frequency only he could hear. Then he began to climb.

Elias didn’t wait. He turned, sprinting across the terrace, his shoes slapping against the stone. A door—wooden, warped with age—stood ajar, its hinges groaning like a dying man. He shouldered it open, the wood splintering under his weight, and stumbled into a hallway lit by a single bare bulb. The air was thick with the scent of garlic and old paper, the walls lined with yellowed photographs and crucifixes.

A home. Someone’s *home*.

He didn’t have time to feel guilt.

The hallway led to a kitchen, its floor tiles cracked and uneven. A pot simmered on the stove, its contents bubbling lazily, steam curling into the air like ghostly fingers. Elias’s stomach growled, the scent of tomatoes and basil a cruel taunt. He grabbed a knife from the block on the counter, its blade serrated, the handle worn smooth by years of use. It was a poor weapon, but it was *something*.

Behind him, the door creaked open.

Vasquez stepped inside, his shoes clicking against the tile. He did not rush. He did not need to. The kitchen was a trap, and Elias was the mouse.

“You’re making this harder than it needs to be,” Vasquez said, his voice a velvet purr. “I don’t enjoy this part.”

Elias’s grip tightened on the knife. “Then don’t do it.”

Vasquez smiled. It was a terrible thing, that smile—all teeth and no warmth. “You know I don’t have a choice.”

The reaper reached into his jacket. Elias didn’t wait to see what he would pull out. He lunged, the knife flashing in the dim light. Vasquez moved like smoke, sidestepping the blade with an ease that made Elias’s stomach clench. The knife grazed his sleeve, slicing through fabric, and for a heartbeat, Elias thought he had him.

Then Vasquez’s hand shot out, his fingers closing around Elias’s wrist like a vise. The knife clattered to the floor.

Elias twisted, driving his knee toward Vasquez’s groin, but the reaper was ready. He pivoted, using Elias’s momentum against him, and suddenly Elias was airborne, his back slamming into the refrigerator with a force that knocked the breath from his lungs. Magnets and photographs rained down around him, the faces of strangers staring up at him in silent accusation.

Vasquez loomed over him, his expression unreadable. “You’re good,” he said, almost admiringly. “But good isn’t enough.”

Elias spat blood onto the floor. “Then what is?”

Vasquez didn’t answer. Instead, he reached into his jacket again, this time pulling out a syringe, its needle glinting in the light. “This is cleaner,” he said. “Less messy.”

Elias’s pulse hammered in his throat. He had seen what that needle did. Had seen men reduced to drooling, twitching husks, their minds unspooled like thread from a bobbin. He would not let that be him.

He kicked out, his foot connecting with Vasquez’s knee. The reaper grunted, his leg buckling, and Elias scrambled to his feet. He grabbed the pot from the stove, its contents scalding his palm, and hurled it at Vasquez’s face. The reaper hissed, stumbling back, hot liquid splashing across his shirt.

Elias didn’t wait. He bolted for the door, his shoulder slamming into the frame as he burst back into the hallway. Behind him, Vasquez roared, the sound raw and animalistic, and Elias knew he had made a mistake.

The reaper was *angry* now.

The hallway stretched before him, a gauntlet of closed doors and flickering light. He tried the first door—locked. The second—locked. The third gave way beneath his weight, and he tumbled into a bedroom, the air thick with the scent of lavender and old perfume. A woman’s dress lay draped over a chair, its fabric shimmering like liquid silver. A vanity stood against the wall, its mirror cracked, the glass spiderwebbed with fractures.

Elias didn’t stop. He crossed the room in three strides, his hand closing around the window latch. The lock was stiff, rusted shut, and he cursed, his fingers slipping as he wrenched at it. Behind him, the bedroom door crashed open.

Vasquez filled the doorway, his shirt stained with sauce, his face a mask of cold fury. “You’re *pissing me off*,” he growled.

Elias didn’t answer. He drove his elbow into the window, the glass shattering outward in a rain of glittering shards. Cold air rushed in, carrying the scent of the sea. Below, the street was a sheer drop, the cobblestones waiting like teeth.

He didn’t hesitate.

He climbed onto the sill, his hands slick with blood, his heart a drumbeat in his ears. Vasquez lunged, his fingers brushing Elias’s ankle, but Elias was already falling, the night swallowing him whole.

The impact knocked the wind from his lungs. He rolled, his body a tangle of limbs, the cobblestones biting into his skin. Pain flared, white-hot, but he forced himself up, his legs unsteady beneath him. The street was empty, the city holding its breath.

Then, from above, a shadow detached itself from the window.

Vasquez landed in a crouch, his suit miraculously unrumpled, his expression unreadable. He stood slowly, his gaze locking onto Elias like a predator scenting blood.

Elias ran.

The street sloped downward, a river of stone, and he followed its current, his breath ragged, his body screaming. The city blurred around him—shuttered shops, graffiti-scarred walls, the skeletal remains of a Christmas tree, its tinsel dulled by time. A cat darted across his path, its eyes glowing like embers in the dark.

Behind him, Vasquez’s footsteps were a metronome, steady and unrelenting.

Elias’s mind raced. *Think. Think. Think.*

The docks. They were close. The docks meant water, meant boats, meant *escape*. But the docks also meant open space, meant no cover, meant Vasquez would have a clear shot.

A risk. But risks were all he had left.

He veered left, his shoes skidding on the slick stones, and burst onto the waterfront. The bay stretched before him, vast and black, the lights of the city reflected on its surface like fallen stars. Fishing boats bobbed at their moorings, their hulls creaking, their masts swaying like drunken sentinels. The air was thick with the scent of salt and diesel, the tang of fish and the sharp, acrid bite of rust.

Elias sprinted toward the nearest boat, a weather-beaten trawler with peeling paint and a name he couldn’t read. He leapt onto the deck, his hands scrambling for purchase on the slick wood. The boat rocked beneath him, the motion disorienting, and for a heartbeat, he thought he would fall.

Then Vasquez was there.

The reaper stood on the dock, his silhouette framed against the city’s glow, his hands loose at his sides. He did not raise his gun. He did not need to.

“You can’t outrun this,” he said, his voice carrying across the water like a curse.

Elias’s fingers closed around a rusted harpoon, its shaft cold and heavy in his grip. “I can try.”

Vasquez sighed, as if disappointed. “You always were a stubborn bastard.”

Then he stepped onto the boat.

The trawler groaned beneath their combined weight, the wood protesting with a sound like a dying animal. Elias didn’t wait. He swung the harpoon, the blade whistling through the air. Vasquez dodged, the harpoon embedding itself in the mast with a *thunk* that vibrated up Elias’s arms.

The reaper lunged.

Elias sidestepped, his body moving on instinct, but Vasquez was faster. His hand shot out, his fingers closing around Elias’s throat, and suddenly the world narrowed to the pressure of those fingers, the way they cut off his air, the way they made his vision swim.

Elias drove his knee into Vasquez’s gut, but the reaper barely flinched. He was a wall of muscle and bone, immovable. Elias’s lungs burned, his vision darkening at the edges. He clawed at Vasquez’s wrist, his nails digging into flesh, but the reaper’s grip only tightened.

“Why?” Elias gasped, the word barely a whisper.

Vasquez leaned in, his breath hot against Elias’s ear. “Because you *know*.”

Then he twisted.

Elias’s world exploded in pain. His vision went white, his body convulsing, and for a heartbeat, he was weightless, suspended in the dark. Then the water rushed up to meet him, cold and merciless, and the bay swallowed him whole.

The last thing he saw was Vasquez’s silhouette, standing on the deck of the trawler, watching him sink.

Then there was only darkness.
