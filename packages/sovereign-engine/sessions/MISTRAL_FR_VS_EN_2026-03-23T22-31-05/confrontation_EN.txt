**The Last Round at the Hollow Pint**

The door groaned like a dying man’s last breath, hinges slick with rain and rust, and the air inside the Hollow Pint hit him like a wet sack of coins—sour mash, stale sweat, the ghost of fried onions clinging to the rafters. Ten years had sharpened the stink, but not enough to dull the memory of it. The bar was a bruise on the highway’s flank, a place where headlights bled into nothing and men came to forget the names of their regrets. Tonight, one of them would remember.

He stepped in slow, boots scuffing the warped pine floorboards, the sound swallowed by the low hum of a jukebox playing something twangy and broken. The bartender, a slab of a man with forearms like knotted rope, didn’t look up from polishing a glass that had seen better decades. The regulars—three of them, hunched over their drinks like vultures over carrion—glanced up, then away. They knew the weight of a man who walked in with a storm behind his eyes.

The stool at the end was empty. He took it.

“Whiskey,” he said. “Neat.”

The bartender poured without a word, the amber liquid catching the dim light like a warning. The glass hit the counter with a thud. “You ain’t from around here.”

“No.”

A beat. The jukebox skipped. Somewhere in the back, a woman laughed, sharp and sudden, like a bottle breaking.

He sipped. The whiskey burned, but not enough. Not like the fire in his chest, the one that had been smoldering since the night his sister’s voice cracked over the phone, since the cops shrugged and said *missing persons cases go cold*, since he’d traced the last call to this godforsaken stretch of nowhere.

Ten years. Ten years of names scratched into bar napkins, of faces blurred by time and liquor, of dead ends and half-truths. But he’d found the trail. A bartender in Cheyenne who remembered a man with a scar like a question mark. A trucker in Omaha who’d seen him drinking alone, muttering about a girl who *should’ve kept her mouth shut*. And then, the final piece: a postcard, yellowed at the edges, postmarked from this very town. *Wish you were here*, it said. No signature. Just a smudge of lipstick, the same shade as the last time he’d seen her smile.

The bartender was watching him now, eyes narrow. “You look like a man with a question.”

“I’m looking for someone.”

“Ain’t we all.”

The man at the far end of the bar—lean, wiry, with a face like a hatchet left too long in the rain—shifted in his seat. His fingers drummed against his glass, a nervous tattoo. The others didn’t move, but the air thickened, like the moment before a gun goes off.

“Name’s Cole,” the wiry man said, voice too smooth, too practiced. “You got a name, stranger?”

The whiskey glass was empty. He didn’t answer. Instead, he reached into his coat and pulled out the postcard, slid it across the counter. The lipstick smudge was a wound.

Cole’s smile didn’t reach his eyes. “Pretty thing. Who’s it from?”

“You tell me.”

A laugh, dry as kindling. “You think I know every girl who’s ever licked a stamp?”

The bartender’s hand disappeared beneath the counter. The other two men straightened, shoulders tense. The jukebox wheezed to a stop.

He leaned in. “I think you know the one who sent it. The one who was supposed to meet me here, ten years ago. The one who never showed.”

Cole’s fingers stilled. For a second, just a second, something flickered in his gaze—fear, maybe, or recognition. Then it was gone, replaced by a smirk that didn’t fit his face. “You got the wrong man, friend. I don’t know what you’re talking about.”

“Don’t you?”

The bartender’s voice cut through the tension, low and rough. “You need to leave, mister.”

He didn’t move. “Not until I hear it from him.”

Cole sighed, like a man humoring a child. “Look, I don’t know who put this bee in your bonnet, but I ain’t the guy you want. I been here six years. Ask anyone.”

A lie. He could smell it, sharp as the whiskey on his tongue. “Funny. The bartender in Cheyenne said you’ve been coming through for a decade. Said you had a scar.” He reached out, fingers brushing the collar of Cole’s shirt. The fabric was thin, worn. Beneath it, the edge of a puckered line, pale as a worm.

Cole’s hand shot up, knocking his away. “You got no right—”

“Where is she?”

“I don’t know!”

“Bullshit.”

The bartender’s hand reappeared, this time holding a shotgun, the barrels twin black holes staring him down. “Last warning.”

He didn’t flinch. “You really want to do this? In front of witnesses?”

Cole’s laugh was a blade unsheathed. “Witnesses? You think these boys are gonna talk?” He jerked his chin toward the others. “They know what happens to men who ask too many questions.”

The man to his left—thick-necked, with a jaw like a shovel—stood, cracking his knuckles. The one on the right, younger, with eyes too bright, too eager, followed suit.

He exhaled. The whiskey had turned to lead in his gut.

“Alright,” he said. “Let’s dance.”

The first punch came from the left, a haymaker that would’ve taken his head off if he hadn’t ducked. The bar erupted. Glass shattered. A stool skidded across the floor. He drove his elbow into the thick-necked man’s ribs, heard the satisfying crack of bone, but the younger one was on him then, fingers clawing at his face. He twisted, slammed the kid’s head into the counter. Blood sprayed the postcard.

Cole was moving, fast for a man built like a scarecrow. He lunged, a knife glinting in his hand. The blade grazed his side, hot and bright. He gritted his teeth, grabbed Cole’s wrist, and twisted until the knife clattered to the floor. The bartender swung the shotgun up, but he was already moving, yanking Cole in front of him like a shield.

“Drop it,” he snarled.

The bartender hesitated. The shotgun wavered.

“You don’t want to do this,” Cole hissed. “You don’t know what you’re messing with.”

“Don’t I?” He pressed his forearm against Cole’s throat, felt the man’s pulse hammering against his skin. “Where is she?”

“I told you—”

“Wrong answer.”

He drove his knee into Cole’s gut. The man doubled over, gasping. The bartender’s finger tightened on the trigger.

“Last chance,” he said. “Where is my sister?”

Cole’s breath came in ragged bursts. Then, finally, a whisper: “Basement.”

The word hung in the air, heavy as a death sentence.

The bartender’s eyes flicked to the door behind the bar, the one with the padlock. The younger man, blood dripping from his nose, made a sound like a wounded animal.

He shoved Cole away. The man crumpled to the floor, coughing. The shotgun was still pointed at his chest.

“Open it,” he said.

The bartender didn’t move.

“Now.”

The lock clicked. The door creaked open, revealing a set of stairs descending into darkness. The smell hit him first—damp, rot, the copper tang of old blood. His stomach lurched.

He took the steps two at a time, the shotgun’s barrel following him down. The basement was small, cramped, lit by a single bulb swinging from the ceiling. The walls were lined with shelves, stocked with dusty bottles and jars. And in the corner, a shape.

A body.

No. Not a body. A woman, curled on a cot, her hair matted, her skin sallow. She stirred at the sound of his footsteps, eyes fluttering open.

“Jesse?”

His breath left him in a rush. Ten years. Ten years of searching, of rage, of grief. And here she was.

Alive.

Her voice was a rasp. “You came.”

The shotgun blast echoed through the basement.

Pain exploded in his shoulder, white-hot. He staggered, caught himself against the wall. The bartender stood at the top of the stairs, smoke curling from the barrel.

“Should’ve left well enough alone,” Cole said, stepping into the light, the knife back in his hand. “Now you get to join her.”

Jesse screamed.

He lunged.

The fight was ugly, desperate. The knife slashed, the shotgun roared, the basement filled with the sounds of breaking glass and grunts of pain. He drove his fist into Cole’s face, felt bone give beneath his knuckles. The bartender swung the shotgun like a club, but he ducked, grabbed a bottle from the shelf, and smashed it against the man’s temple. The bartender crumpled.

Cole was on him again, the knife flashing. He twisted, felt the blade bite into his arm. Blood dripped onto the floor, mixing with the whiskey and the dust.

“Why?” he gasped. “Why her?”

Cole’s face was a mask of blood and hate. “She knew too much.”

“About what?”

“About *them*.” Cole spat the word like it was poison. “The ones who pay. The ones who *own* this town.”

The ones who’d let a girl disappear and never asked why.

He saw red.

The knife clattered to the floor. Cole’s back hit the wall. His hands closed around the man’s throat, thumbs pressing into the soft flesh beneath his jaw. Cole’s eyes bulged, his fingers clawing at his wrists.

“You should’ve run,” he growled.

Cole’s lips moved, but no sound came out.

Then, a voice—weak, but clear.

“Jesse. *Stop.*”

He turned. Jesse was sitting up, her face pale, her eyes wide. “Don’t,” she whispered. “He’s not worth it.”

His hands trembled. The rage was still there, a living thing inside him, but her voice cut through it like a blade.

He let go.

Cole slumped to the floor, gasping.

Jesse reached for him, her fingers brushing his arm. “You found me.”

He pulled her into his arms, her body fragile against his. “I promised I would.”

The sirens were distant at first, then closer. Someone must’ve called the cops. The bartender groaned, stirring. Cole lay still, his chest rising and falling in shallow breaths.

Jesse’s voice was a whisper. “What now?”

He looked at the stairs, at the blood on the floor, at the man who’d stolen ten years of her life.

“Now,” he said, “we go home.”

The shotgun lay at his feet. He picked it up, checked the chamber. One shell left.

Cole’s eyes widened. “You wouldn’t.”

He smiled. It wasn’t a kind smile.

Then he turned and walked away.
