**The Platform at Midnight**

The last train exhaled into the dark, its steel lungs collapsing with a sigh that curled around the empty platform like smoke from a dying cigarette. Clara stood where it had left her—where she had chosen to remain—her fingers still warm from the ghost of the handrail, the metal’s residual pulse thrumming against her skin as if the train had taken a piece of her with it. The air smelled of wet stone and distant diesel, of something older, something like the inside of a grandfather clock left to rust in the rain. She breathed it in, slow, deliberate, as though the night itself were a language she was learning to speak.

*Why didn’t you get on?*

The question had been asked in a voice like crushed velvet, low and laced with something that might have been amusement or pity—she couldn’t decide which. It had come from Daniel, of course, because only Daniel would have noticed, would have cared enough to ask in that way that made her feel both seen and dissected. They had been standing in the kitchen of his flat, the one with the yellow walls that always made her think of butter left too long in the sun, the kind that turns waxy and sad. She had been packing a bag—just a small one, the kind you take when you’re not sure if you’re leaving or just pretending to—and he had watched her with that half-smile, the one that suggested he already knew the answer but wanted to hear her say it anyway.

*I don’t know,* she had lied, because the truth was a tangle of things she couldn’t unravel: the way the train’s departure had felt like a door clicking shut behind her, the way the platform’s emptiness had suddenly seemed like the only honest thing in the world, the way she had stood there, paralyzed, while the numbers on the departure board flickered and died like fireflies in a jar.

Now, the platform stretched before her, a tongue of concrete licking at the void. The overhead lights buzzed, their fluorescence leaching the color from everything—the benches, the graffitied trash cans, her own hands, which looked suddenly foreign, as if they belonged to someone else. She flexed her fingers, watched the tendons shift beneath her skin. *Whose hands are these?* she thought, and the question settled in her chest like a stone.

She remembered the way Daniel had reached for her wrist that night, his thumb pressing into the pulse point where her blood ran fastest, as if he could read her heartbeat like Braille. *You’re not running away,* he had said, not a question, not quite an accusation. *You’re just… waiting for the right train to miss.* And she had laughed, because it was easier than admitting he was right, easier than explaining that some absences were louder than presences, that the space between departure and arrival was the only place she had ever felt truly alone.

The wind picked up, slithering between the gaps in her coat, carrying with it the scent of rain that hadn’t yet fallen. She shivered, but not from the cold. It was the kind of shiver that started in the bones and worked its way outward, the kind that made her teeth ache. She thought of the last time she had been this still, this aware of her own body—it had been in a museum, standing before a painting of a woman in a blue dress, the brushstrokes so thick they looked like they might drip if you touched them. The woman in the painting had been looking over her shoulder, her face half in shadow, as if she were listening for something just out of frame. Clara had stared at her for so long that her vision had blurred, and for a moment, she had been convinced the woman was looking back.

*What do you see when you look at her?* Daniel had asked, his voice soft, almost reverent.

*Someone who’s already decided to leave,* Clara had said, and then, because she couldn’t help herself: *Someone who’s already gone.*

He had frowned at that, his brow furrowing in that way it always did when she said something that unsettled him. *You talk about her like she’s real,* he had said.

*She is,* Clara had replied, because wasn’t she? Wasn’t the woman in the painting more real than the people who walked past her every day without seeing her at all?

Now, the platform’s silence pressed in around her, thick as wool. She closed her eyes and listened to the hum of the city at night—the distant wail of a siren, the growl of an engine, the occasional clatter of a shopping cart being pushed by someone who, like her, had nowhere better to be. She wondered if the woman in the painting ever heard these sounds, if she ever stood on a platform like this one, waiting for a train that would never come.

*You’re doing it again,* Daniel had said once, catching her staring at nothing, her mind drifting like a boat untethered. *You’re here, but you’re not here.*

*Where else would I be?* she had asked, though she knew what he meant.

*Anywhere,* he had said. *Everywhere. Nowhere.*

She opened her eyes. The platform was still empty, but the air felt different now, charged, as if the night had taken a breath and was holding it. She thought of the way Daniel had kissed her the last time they had stood like this, in the half-dark of his bedroom, the curtains drawn against the world. It had been a kiss that tasted of coffee and something bitter, something like regret. *Stay,* he had whispered against her mouth, but she had already been pulling away, already reaching for the door.

*I can’t,* she had said, and it wasn’t a lie, not really. It was just the truth, stripped bare.

Now, the platform stretched before her, a liminal space, a threshold. She took a step forward, then another, her shoes scuffing against the concrete. The lights above her flickered, once, twice, as if the night itself were blinking. She thought of the woman in the painting, of the way her gaze had seemed to follow Clara no matter where she stood. *What are you waiting for?* the woman had seemed to ask, and Clara had no answer, not then, not now.

She reached the edge of the platform and looked down at the tracks, at the gravel and the ties and the third rail, humming with latent power. She wondered what it would feel like to step off, to let the night swallow her whole. Would it be like falling, or like flying? Would it hurt, or would it feel like coming home?

*You’re not serious,* Daniel had said when she had told him, half-joking, that she sometimes thought about it—the weightlessness of it, the finality. *You’re not that kind of person.*

*What kind of person am I?* she had asked, but he hadn’t answered, and she hadn’t pressed him, because she already knew. She was the kind of person who stood on empty platforms at midnight, the kind of person who let trains leave without her, the kind of person who was always waiting for something that never came.

The wind picked up again, colder this time, and she wrapped her arms around herself, her coat suddenly too thin, too insubstantial. She thought of the last time she had felt warm—it had been in Daniel’s bed, the sheets tangled around them, his breath hot against her neck. *You’re shivering,* he had said, pulling her closer, and she had let him, because it was easier than telling him the truth: that she wasn’t cold, not really. That what she felt was something deeper, something like hunger, something like grief.

Now, the platform was a stage, and she was the only actor left. She thought of the woman in the painting again, of the way her dress had seemed to ripple even though there was no wind, no movement at all. *What do you want?* the woman had seemed to ask, and Clara had wanted to scream, because she didn’t know, she never knew. She wanted to be seen, she wanted to disappear. She wanted to stay, she wanted to leave. She wanted to be the kind of person who knew what she wanted.

The lights flickered again, and this time, they stayed off. The platform plunged into darkness, and for a moment, Clara was blind, her breath coming fast, her heart hammering against her ribs. Then her eyes adjusted, and she saw the city beyond the tracks, its lights winking like distant stars. She thought of Daniel, of the way he had looked at her that last night, his eyes dark with something she couldn’t name. *You’ll come back,* he had said, but it hadn’t been a question, and she hadn’t answered, because she didn’t know if it was true.

She took another step forward, her toes brushing the edge of the platform. The tracks gleamed in the dark, slick with rain that hadn’t yet fallen. She thought of the train that had left without her, of the people on it, their faces blurred by speed and distance. She wondered where they were going, if any of them had ever stood where she was standing now, if any of them had ever felt this same pull, this same vertigo.

*You’re not alone,* Daniel had said once, his voice rough with something like anger. *You just act like you are.*

She had wanted to tell him that it wasn’t an act, that the loneliness was real, that it lived inside her like a second heart, beating in time with her own. But she hadn’t, because she knew he wouldn’t understand. He was the kind of person who filled silences, who reached for her hand when she pulled away. He was the kind of person who believed in answers.

She wasn’t.

The wind howled, a sound like a train approaching, but when she turned, there was nothing there, just the empty tracks, the empty night. She thought of the woman in the painting again, of the way her face had seemed to shift when you looked at it from different angles, as if she were many women at once. *Who are you?* Clara had wanted to ask, but the painting had no answers, only questions.

She took a deep breath, the cold air burning her lungs. She thought of Daniel’s hands, of the way they had felt on her skin, rough and warm and alive. She thought of the last train, of the way it had pulled away from the platform without her, its lights receding into the dark. She thought of the woman in the painting, of the way she had seemed to be waiting for something, just like Clara was waiting now.

And then she stepped back, away from the edge, because she wasn’t ready, not yet. The platform was still empty, the night still endless. She turned and walked toward the exit, her footsteps echoing in the dark. She didn’t know where she was going, only that she had to keep moving, had to keep walking, had to keep waiting for the next train, the next departure, the next moment when everything would change.

But for now, there was only the platform, the night, the silence. And Clara, standing in the dark, listening for a train that would never come.
