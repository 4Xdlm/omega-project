The door yielded to her trembling hand with that peculiar reluctance which doors possess when they guard the chambers of the departed, as though the very hinges had grown solemn in their office and would admit the living only with the gravest ceremony. There, before her, lay the apartment in all its terrible fidelity—a museum of mortality where every stick of furniture stood sentinel over memories, where the very air itself seemed thick with the perfume of lavender water and that indefinable essence which clings to places where love has long resided and suddenly fled.

How cruelly unchanged it all appeared! The antimacassar draped just so upon the horsehair sofa, that monument to respectability which had witnessed a thousand small domestic dramas; the china shepherdess upon the mantelpiece, chipped at the elbow where it had suffered some long-forgotten catastrophe, yet smiling still with painted serenity; the carpet, worn thin in that precise path between window and hearth where her mother's slippered feet had traced their daily pilgrimage for thirty years or more.

With what reluctant courage she approached the escritoire—that faithful confidant of feminine secrets—and drew forth the topmost drawer, which protested with a squeak of recognition. Here lay the archaeology of affection: letters tied with ribbon faded from rose to the colour of old tears, daguerreotypes staring up with the solemn intensity that photography demands of its subjects, and there, beneath a prayer book whose pages fell open of their own accord to the Twenty-third Psalm, a notebook bound in morocco leather, its spine creased with handling.

The letters spoke in voices she had thought forever silenced—her father's careful copperplate declaring his devotion across the years, her own childish scrawl reporting the triumphs and disasters of the schoolroom. Each photograph was a resurrection: here stood her grandmother, stern as judgment in her widow's weeds, there her brother as a boy, gap-toothed and grinning, claimed by fever before his voice could break.

But it was the notebook that proved her undoing, for there, in her mother's familiar hand, were recorded not the grand events of history, but the small, precious particulars of love: "Margaret spoke her first word today—'Mama'—though she has been saying it in her sleep for weeks," and "Made her favourite pudding for her birthday, though she pretends now she is too sophisticated for such nursery fare."

The tears came then, not as she had expected them—in a tempest of grief—but quietly, steadily, like rain upon the roof of memory, each drop a small acknowledgment that love, once given, never truly dies, but lives on in the secret places of the heart, in the scent of lavender, in the faithful keeping of small, sacred things.
