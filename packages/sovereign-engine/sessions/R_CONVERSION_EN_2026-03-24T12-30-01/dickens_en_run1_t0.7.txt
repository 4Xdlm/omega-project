In that most melancholy of establishments—a café bereft of its customary bustle, where the very chairs seemed to huddle together for warmth like orphaned children in a workhouse dormitory—there sat a gentleman whose countenance bore the peculiar mixture of hope and dread that marks a man standing upon the precipice of his own destiny. The rain, that most democratic of London's visitors, drummed its relentless tattoo upon the windows with all the persistence of a bailiff at a debtor's door, each drop a liquid messenger bearing news of the tempest that raged not merely in the heavens above, but in the chambers of the waiting man's heart.

Mr. Cornelius Blackthorne—for such was his name, though he had worn it these forty-three years with no more distinction than a clerk might wear his threadbare coat—consulted his timepiece with the nervous frequency of a condemned man counting the hours to his appointment with eternity. The brass face of the watch, inherited from a father whose chief legacy had been disappointment and unpaid bills, reflected the gaslight with a wan gleam that seemed to mock the very notion of punctuality in a world where Fate herself kept such irregular hours.

The café, that temple to the twin gods of caffeine and conversation, stood as empty as a miser's heart, its marble-topped tables bearing only the ghostly impressions of departed cups and the faint perfume of a thousand whispered confidences. The proprietor, a stout fellow whose mustache drooped with the weight of accumulated sorrows and unpaid rent, had retreated behind his counter like a general surveying a battlefield after the rout, polishing glasses with the mechanical devotion of a penitent telling his beads.

Oh, but what strange alchemy was this, that transformed the very air of the place into something thick with possibility! For Blackthorne knew—knew with that peculiar certainty that visits men in their darkest hours like an angel bearing tidings both terrible and wonderful—that the next soul to cross that threshold would prove the architect of his transformation. Whether toward salvation or damnation, toward the sunlit uplands of prosperity or the shadowed valleys of ruin, he could not say; but change would come as surely as the Thames flows toward the sea, carrying with it the detritus of a thousand dreams and the seeds of a thousand more.

The rain intensified, as if Nature herself were weeping for all the lives about to be altered, all the comfortable certainties about to be shattered like so much china in a bull's rampage, and still Blackthorne waited, his soul suspended between the tick and the tock of time's inexorable march.
