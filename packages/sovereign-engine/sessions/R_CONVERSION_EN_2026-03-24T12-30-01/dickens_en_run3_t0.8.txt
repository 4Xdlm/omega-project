The doors swung shut behind her with that peculiar whisper which belongs only to the chambers where life and death conduct their eternal negotiations, and Dr. Arabella Thornfield—for she had fought tooth and nail against a world that would deny her the title—found herself expelled into the corridor like some modern Jonah cast up upon the shore of her own making. The hallway stretched before and behind her, a tunnel of such desolate whiteness that it might have been carved from the very bones of disappointment itself, its gas-lamps flickering with the uncertain pulse of a conscience that knows it has witnessed too much.

How the walls seemed to lean inward, those silent witnesses dressed in their institutional paint the color of old porridge, bearing upon their surfaces the accumulated sighs of ten thousand souls who had paced these same stones! The very air hung thick with the ghosts of other failures, other triumphs, other moments when mortal hands had reached into the sacred mystery of the human frame and found themselves, despite all learning and intention, merely fumbling in the dark.

Against this sepulchral backdrop she pressed her spine—that same spine which had borne her through the medical colleges where professors looked upon her as they might regard a dancing bear, curious but ultimately convinced that nature had played some cosmic jest—and felt the cold seep through her surgical gown like the judgment of eternity itself. Her hands, those instruments which had just moments before held a beating heart as tenderly as a mother cradles her infant, now trembled with the particular palsy that visits those who have gazed too long into the abyss of their own limitations.

Behind her closed eyelids, she could still see young Margaret Fairweather lying upon the table, her chest rising and falling with the mechanical persistence of a broken clock that insists on marking time though its face has been shattered. Alive, yes—blessed be that small mercy!—but diminished, reduced, like a once-grand melody played now upon a piano missing half its keys. The tumor had been too close to the vital vessels, too intimately acquainted with the very pipes through which life flows, and in saving the girl's breath she had been forced to sacrifice something ineffable, some spark that no medical text had ever catalogued yet which every surgeon learns to recognize by its absence.

The corridor stretched on, indifferent as time itself, while she stood there like a statue erected to commemorate the eternal struggle between human ambition and divine mystery—a monument to the terrible weight of holding another's mortality in one's palm, and discovering that even the most skillful fingers sometimes prove too clumsy for the delicate work of playing God.
