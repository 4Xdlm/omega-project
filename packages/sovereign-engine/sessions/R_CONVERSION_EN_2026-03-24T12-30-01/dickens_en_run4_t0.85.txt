The cobblestones, those ancient witnesses to ten thousand footfalls, stretched before old Jeremiah Weatherby like a grey tongue lolling from the mouth of memory itself—familiar yet foreign, worn smooth by decades of boots that had carried away his youth, his friends, his very soul, leaving only the stones to remember what once was. How they gleamed with that peculiar dampness which England bestows upon her villages like a benediction of melancholy! The houses stood sentinel still, their Tudor beams black as mourning bands against whitewashed walls that had grown dingy with the accumulated sighs of departed generations, each window staring blindly at this bent figure who moved among them like a ghost seeking absolution from other ghosts.

Mrs. Hartwell's cottage—ah, but it was Mrs. Hartwell's no longer!—squatted beneath its thatched burden with the weary resignation of an old woman whose children have all emigrated to America, its garden gate hanging askew like a broken jaw, the roses that once bloomed there with such magnificent audacity now replaced by nettles that whispered accusations in the autumn wind. The very air tasted different, seasoned as it was with the absence of familiar voices: where was Tom Brightwater's robust laughter that once echoed from the smithy? Where the gentle scolding of Miss Pennyfeather as she shooed children from her apple trees? Gone, all gone, swept away by time's inexorable broom, leaving only the architecture of remembrance and the bitter-sweet perfume of decay.

He paused before the village green—that emerald stage where his boyhood had performed its daily comedies and tragedies—now bisected by a modern road that sliced through childhood like a surgeon's knife through a beating heart, and there, beneath the ancient oak whose branches had once sheltered sweethearts' promises and children's games, he sought what he could not name: perhaps the echo of his own young voice calling across the years, perhaps the phantom touch of Sarah Millfield's hand in his, perhaps simply the assurance that he had once been young, once been loved, once been part of this tapestry that now seemed woven of gossamer and regret.

The oak remembered—trees always do—its gnarled bark scarred with initials carved by hands long since returned to dust, but even the tree seemed smaller now, shrunken by the weight of solitary knowledge, its roots clutching desperately at soil that had drunk the tears of too many partings. Old Jeremiah pressed his palm against its trunk and felt the slow, patient heartbeat of centuries, and in that moment understood with crystalline clarity that what he sought was not here, had never been here, could never be here—for it lived not in this place of stone and timber and earth, but in the country of memory, that distant realm where the dead dance eternal quadrilles and the young never grow old, and love letters written in disappearing ink remain forever legible to those who know the secret of reading with the heart.
