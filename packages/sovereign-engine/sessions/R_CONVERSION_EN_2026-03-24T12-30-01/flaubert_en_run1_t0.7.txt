The café stretched before him like a tomb.

Henri pressed his palm against the marble table, feeling its coldness seep through his skin while the rain, that merciless autumn rain which had been falling since dawn, drummed its monotonous refrain against the tall windows that looked out onto the deserted boulevard where not even the most determined pedestrian dared venture forth, and where the gas lamps, already lit despite the early hour, cast their trembling reflections upon the wet cobblestones like so many fallen stars drowning in earthly puddles. The waiter had long since retreated to some hidden recess of the establishment, leaving Henri alone with his untouched coffee, its surface now bearing that thin, oily film which forms when hot liquid surrenders to the inevitable cooling that time imposes upon all things, much as passion cools, much as certainty dissolves into the gray uncertainty that now gripped his chest with fingers more insistent than any lover's caress.

His watch ticked.

From his waistcoat pocket, he withdrew the silver timepiece that had belonged to his father, that stern man who had never known a moment's doubt about anything, who had moved through life with the confident stride of one who believes the world exists solely to confirm his expectations, and Henri studied the delicate hands that marked not merely the passage of minutes but the inexorable approach of an encounter which would, he knew with the terrible certainty that sometimes visits us like a fever, transform the very foundations upon which he had built his careful, measured existence. The hands pointed to twenty-three minutes past four, and she—for it was a woman, though he had never seen her face, had never heard her voice, knew her only through a series of letters written in a hand so elegant it seemed to dance across the page like music made visible—would arrive at the half-hour.

Thunder rolled overhead.

What manner of revelation awaited him, what species of transformation would she bring, what doors would swing open or slam shut forever when she crossed the threshold of this dim sanctuary where he now sat like a penitent awaiting absolution or damnation? He could not say, could not even imagine, for there are moments in a man's life when the future becomes as opaque as the rain-streaked glass before him, when all his careful plans and reasonable expectations crumble like ancient parchment exposed to flame, leaving him suspended between what was and what might be, between the known country of his past and the undiscovered territory that beckoned with equal measures of promise and menace.

The door would open soon.

And Henri, pressing his palm more firmly against the cold marble, feeling the rain's rhythm merge with the beating of his heart, waited for his life to begin anew, waited for the stranger who would make him a stranger to himself.
