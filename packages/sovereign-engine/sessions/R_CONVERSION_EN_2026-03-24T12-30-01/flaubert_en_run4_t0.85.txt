The road descended.

Through the familiar curve where the elm had once stood—now a stump blackened by some winter's fury—Baptiste Morel advanced with the measured gait of a man who carries within his bones the weight of thirty years' absence, his walking stick striking the cobblestones in a rhythm that echoed against the shuttered windows like a question repeated endlessly to walls that had forgotten how to answer. The houses pressed against one another as they always had, their Norman timber-framing dark with age, their slate roofs catching the pale October light in the same slanted planes that had once sheltered his sleep, yet something indefinable had shifted in their arrangement, as though each dwelling had turned slightly inward during his long exile, closing itself against the possibility of return.

He stopped before the baker's shop.

The sign still swung on its iron chains, still bore the name Dubois in faded letters, but through the window he glimpsed shelves empty of everything save dust motes that danced in the afternoon sun with the aimless persistence of memories seeking their proper places in a mind grown uncertain of its own geography. Where was the counter behind which Madame Dubois had stood each morning, her flour-dusted hands counting out the warm loaves while her husband's voice boomed from the back room, calling out the day's gossip with the authority of a man whose ovens were the village heart? The floorboards, worn smooth by generations of feet seeking their daily bread, stretched bare and silent beneath Baptiste's gaze, offering him nothing but the hollow echo of his own breathing and the terrible certainty that time, in its relentless passage, had swept away not merely the familiar faces but the very possibility that those faces might return.

Something pulled at him.

Not nostalgia—for that sweet melancholy belonged to younger men who still believed in the permanence of their attachments—but rather the inexorable compulsion of a compass needle seeking its true north, drawing him past the church where the bells hung mute in their tower, past the fountain where no children gathered to sail paper boats in the basin's shallow water, past the inn where the windows gazed blindly upon the empty square like the eyes of a sleeper who has forgotten how to wake. What was it he sought in these familiar-strange streets, what fragment of his former self lay buried beneath the accumulated sediment of years spent in cities whose names had once held the promise of transformation but had delivered only the gradual erosion of certainty?

The cemetery gate stood open.

Perhaps there, among the weathered stones and the careful rows of the definitively departed, he might discover what the living village had failed to yield: some tangible proof that his seventy-three years had been more than mere duration, that the boy who had run laughing through these very streets had existed with sufficient force to leave an impression deeper than footprints in mud, more lasting than breath upon glass.

He entered.
